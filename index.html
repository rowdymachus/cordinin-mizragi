Myth // Intel

W1Why myth? Versions, sources, afterlives

There is no ‘original’ myth. Week 1 proves it with one woman, Ariadne, told four different ways. Your exam habit from now on: which version, by whom, when, and why?

The four Ariadnes

Key ideas

  • A myth is a family of versions, not one text. Each version serves its genre, audience and politics.
  • Sources come in layers: epic (Homer, Hesiod) → lyric and tragedy (Pindar, Aeschylus, Euripides) → Hellenistic and Roman poets (Apollonius, Ovid) → mythographers and biographers (Apollodorus, Plutarch).
  • Plutarch's Peisistratus story shows myths were edited for political reasons. Athens wanted a respectable Theseus.
  • Afterlives: Chaucer's Legend of Good Women (Ariadne as a betrayed woman); Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream 2.1, where Oberon lists the women Theseus abandoned, Ariadne among them.
What they’ll askThis is the prototype for the ‘comparison of versions’ question. The structure: what stays the same (Crete, Theseus, Dia, Dionysus) → what changes (her fate, Dionysus' role, tone) → why it changes (genre, period, purpose).
W2What is myth? Greek critics, modern theories, Anatolia

The Greeks were already attacking their own myths in the 6th century BC. This week gives you the ancient critics, the modern theories, and the map of Anatolian cultures that the rest of the course keeps coming back to.

Modern theories (one line each)

  • Aetiology: myth explains why something is so (why we burn bones for the gods → Mecone).
  • Nature myth (Max Müller, 19th c.): myths as garbled stories about sun, sky and dawn. Mostly dead now.
  • Myth and ritual (Frazer, Jane Harrison): myth is the story told alongside a ritual (Demeter and Eleusis).
  • Charter theory (Malinowski): myth legitimises institutions and power (Zeus' kingship; Athens using Theseus).
  • Psychological (Freud, Jung; Otto Rank's hero pattern): myth expresses the unconscious and archetypes.
  • Structuralism (Lévi-Strauss; Vernant): meaning sits in systems of oppositions (gods/humans/beasts, raw/cooked).
  • Narrative (Propp): stories share fixed functions and patterns (the hero pattern).
  • Ideological / political: who benefits from this version? (Peisistratus editing Homer.)
  • Ancient approaches too: allegory (Theagenes of Rhegium), rationalism (Palaephatus), Euhemerism (Euhemerus: gods were great kings who were later worshipped).

Bremmer, “What Is a Greek Myth?”

  • Definition to memorise: myths are “traditional tales relevant to society.”
  • In Homer, mythos means authoritative speech, not ‘false story’. The myth vs logos opposition comes later, with philosophers and historians.
  • Greek myth had no fixed canon or holy book. It lived in rituals, festivals, poetry and vase painting, and kept changing.

W3Creation I: the birth of the cosmos

Two creation stories that work completely differently. In Hesiod the world is born, through sex, family and violence. In Ovid the world is sorted out of chaos by a calm craftsman god. Herodotus tells you why Hesiod carries so much authority.

Hesiod, Theogony 1–232, step by step

  • 1–115 Hymn to the Muses of Helicon. They meet Hesiod while he herds lambs, give him a laurel staff and a divine voice. Lines 27–28: they know how to tell many lies that look like truth, and how to tell the truth when they want. The poet admits myth can deceive.
  • 116–122 First comes Chaos (a gap or void, not a mess), then Gaia (Earth), Tartarus, and Eros (desire, needed so that everything can breed).
  • 123–132 Chaos → Erebus and Night; Night → Aether and Day. Gaia alone → Ouranos (Sky), the mountains, Pontus (Sea).
  • 133–153 Gaia + Ouranos → 12 Titans (Oceanus, Coeus, Crius, Hyperion, Iapetus, Theia, Rhea, Themis, Mnemosyne, Phoebe, Tethys, and Kronos, the youngest, ‘crooked-minded’); 3 Cyclopes (Brontes, Steropes, Arges); 3 Hundred-Handers (Cottus, Briareus, Gyges).
  • 154–210 Ouranos pushes the children back inside Gaia. She makes a sickle of adamant; Kronos ambushes and castrates Ouranos. His blood on the Earth → Erinyes, Giants, Meliae (ash-tree nymphs). The genitals fall into the sea → foam (aphros) → Aphrodite, who passes Cythera and comes ashore at Cyprus.
  • 211–232 Children of Night (Doom, Death, Sleep, Dreams, Blame, the Fates, Nemesis, Deceit, Old Age, Strife) and of Strife/Eris (Toil, Famine, Pains, Battles, Murders, Lies, Lawlessness, Oath). Abstractions are gods too. Evil is built into the cosmos from the start.

Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.1–88

  • Opening: he will sing of bodies changed into new forms, from the world's first beginning down to his own time (Augustus).
  • Chaos is a rough, unordered mass where hot fights cold and wet fights dry.
  • ‘Some god, whoever he was’ (or a kinder Nature) ends the fight: separates earth from sky and water, sets up climate zones, winds, stars, animals.
  • Humans come last: made either from divine seed or by Prometheus mixing earth with rainwater, in the image of the gods. Animals look down at the ground; humans stand upright to look at the sky.

Herodotus 2.53 and West

  • Herodotus 2.53: Homer and Hesiod lived about 400 years before him and were the ones who gave the Greeks their theogony: the gods' names, honours, skills and appearances. Greek religion had no holy book, only poets. (In 2.50 he claims many god-names came from Egypt.)
  • M. L. West's introduction: Hesiod c. 700 BC, living at Ascra in Boeotia; his father came from Cyme in Aeolis (the Anatolian coast). The Theogony mixes hymn, catalogue and succession myth. West stresses the Near Eastern parallels.
What they’ll askThe Muses' ‘lies like truth’ lines and the castration scene are classic commentary passages. Plato attacks exactly this castration story in Republic 377e–378a, so link them.
W4Creation II: kingship in heaven and its Anatolian relatives

The succession myth: a line of sky-kings, each overthrown by his son, until Zeus breaks the cycle. The same pattern turns up in Hittite songs from Hattusa (Boğazkale, Çorum) and in the Babylonian creation epic. This week is the core of the ‘parallel or influence?’ question.

Hesiod: the rest of the succession

  • 453–506 Kronos swallows his children (Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades, Poseidon) because a son is fated to overthrow him. Rhea gives him a stone in swaddling clothes; Zeus is hidden in Crete (Lyctus). Kronos vomits up the children and the stone, which Zeus sets up at Pytho (Delphi). Zeus frees the Cyclopes, who give him thunder and lightning.
  • 617–735 Titanomachy: ten years of war. Zeus frees the Hundred-Handers and feeds them nectar and ambrosia. They throw rocks, Zeus throws thunderbolts, the Titans go down to Tartarus: an anvil would fall nine days from sky to earth and nine more from earth to Tartarus. The Hundred-Handers guard them.
  • 820–880 Typhoeus, the last child of Gaia (with Tartarus): a hundred snake heads, the voices of every animal. Zeus burns him and throws him into Tartarus; he becomes the source of destructive winds.
  • 881–929 The gods ask Zeus to be king (on Gaia's advice); he distributes honours (timai). He marries Metis and swallows her while she's pregnant so no son can replace him; Athena is born from his head. Then Themis (Seasons, Fates), Eurynome (Graces), Demeter (Persephone), Mnemosyne (Muses), Leto (Apollo, Artemis), Hera (Hebe, Ares, Eileithyia). Hera produces Hephaestus alone.
  • Point: Zeus wins by force (thunderbolt, allies), intelligence (he literally swallows Metis, ‘cunning’) and a fair distribution of honours. That's why the cycle stops with him.

Apollodorus, Library 1.1–1.2 and 1.6.3

  • Ouranos is the first ruler and throws the Cyclopes and Hundred-Handers into Tartarus. Castration, then Kronos rules. Zeus is born in a cave on Dikte (Crete) and nursed by Amaltheia; the Curetes clash spears to cover his crying. Metis gives Kronos a drug that makes him vomit the children. After the war, Zeus, Poseidon and Hades draw lots: sky, sea, underworld.
  • 1.6.3 Typhon, the Anatolian version: born in Cilicia. The gods flee to Egypt disguised as animals. Zeus fights him at Mount Casius (Syria). Typhon cuts out Zeus' sinews with the sickle and carries him to the Corycian cave in Cilicia (near Silifke, Mersin), hiding the sinews in a bearskin guarded by the she-dragon Delphyne. Hermes and Aegipan steal them back. The Fates trick Typhon with ‘ephemeral fruits’. On Mount Haemus his blood (haima) names the mountain. Finally Zeus throws Mount Etna on him.

Burkert, “Oriental and Greek Mythology: The Meeting of Parallels”

  • Some parallels are too close to be chance: Kumarbi/Kronos; the world divided by lot in Iliad 15 ↔ the Babylonian Atrahasis (Anu, Enlil, Enki); Oceanus and Tethys as first parents (Iliad 14) ↔ Apsu and Tiamat.
  • Route: contact in the 8th–7th c. BC (the ‘orientalising’ period) through Phoenician and North Syrian traders, Cilicia, Cyprus, Al Mina; maybe wandering seers and craftsmen.
  • Method: a parallel is not influence. To argue influence you need (1) a specific shared detail, not just ‘a god is king’, (2) a plausible route, (3) dates that fit.

Hamilton and Graves (secondary retellings)

  • Hamilton: a clean, sanitised retelling based mostly on Hesiod and Ovid. Fine for plot, useless as a source.
  • Graves: retells the Olympian creation myth but reads Greek myth as coded history (an older goddess-centred religion overthrown by patriarchal invaders). Speculative, and most scholars reject it. Cite both as retellings, never as ancient sources.
What they’ll ask‘Distinguish parallel from influence’ is literally one of his learning outcomes. Put Burkert's three tests in your essay and check each parallel against them. Bonus: Typhon's whole story happens in Cilicia, which is what the course description calls ‘the Cilician Typhon’.
W5Creation III: Prometheus and Pandora

Why are humans the way they are: burning bones for the gods, needing fire, working the fields, marrying, dying? Hesiod answers with Prometheus' tricks and Zeus' revenge, which is woman. Aeschylus and Plato take the same story and turn it into a hymn to progress and a theory of politics.

Hesiod, Theogony 507–616

  • Prometheus (son of Iapetus, brother of Atlas, Menoetius, Epimetheus) at Mecone, when gods and humans were being separated, divides an ox: meat and fatty innards hidden in the ugly stomach vs bones wrapped in shiny fat. Zeus sees the trick but picks the bones, and that's why humans burn bones for the gods (aetiology of sacrifice).
  • Zeus withholds fire; Prometheus steals it in a hollow fennel stalk.
  • Zeus' counter-gift: the first woman. Hephaestus moulds her from earth, Athena dresses her. She is a ‘beautiful evil’ (kalon kakon). From her comes the ‘race of women’, compared to drones that eat what the bees work for. Not named Pandora here, and there is no jar.
  • Prometheus is chained; an eagle eats his liver every day; Heracles later kills the eagle (with Zeus' approval, for Heracles' glory).
  • Moral: you cannot deceive the mind of Zeus.

Hesiod, Works and Days: Pandora and the Five Ages

  • Here she is named Pandora (‘all gifts’): every god gives her something, Hermes gives her a lying mind. Epimetheus accepts her despite Prometheus' warning. She opens the jar (pithos). The ‘box’ comes from a 16th-century mistranslation by Erasmus. Evils fly out; only Hope (Elpis) stays inside.
  • Five Ages: Gold (under Kronos), Silver, Bronze, Heroes (the only non-metal one: Thebes and Troy), Iron (now: toil, and it will get worse).

Aeschylus and Plato

  • *Aeschylus, Prometheus Bound 436–506: Prometheus lists his gifts. Before him humans saw without seeing and lived in caves like ants. He gave them astronomy and the seasons, numbers, writing (memory), yoked animals, ships, medicine, prophecy, metals. Final idea: all human arts come from Prometheus. Prometheus as culture hero and rebel; Zeus as a new tyrant.* The opposite of Hesiod.
  • *Plato, Protagoras 320c–322d: the gods shape creatures from earth and fire. Epimetheus hands out abilities to the animals (speed, claws, fur) and runs out before he reaches humans, who are left naked. Prometheus steals technical skill plus fire from Hephaestus and Athena. Humans survive but can't live in cities; they wrong each other. Zeus sends Hermes with respect (aidōs) and justice (dikē*), given to everyone equally. That's a justification for democracy: everyone gets a say in politics.

Vernant, “The Myth of Prometheus in Hesiod” (structuralist)

  • Read the Theogony and Works and Days versions together, as one system.
  • Mecone fixes the human place between gods and beasts: gods don't need food and never die; beasts eat raw; humans sacrifice, cook, eat, and die.
  • Linked gifts and losses: sacrificial meat, fire (has to be kept and kindled), woman and marriage (reproduction), and grain hidden in the earth (farming = work).
  • Everything runs on deception (dolos): an appearance hides its opposite. The ugly stomach hides meat, the shiny fat hides bones, the beautiful woman hides a hungry belly (gaster).

English afterlives

  • Byron, “Prometheus” (1816). P. B. Shelley, Prometheus Unbound (1820): Prometheus as a revolutionary, freed while Jupiter falls. Mary Shelley, Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus (1818). Milton compares Eve to Pandora (Paradise Lost 4.714–719).

W6The reign of the Olympians: Zeus and the pantheon as a system

After the wars, a system: Zeus on top, managing a family of quarrelling powers. The Iliad passages show how it works: threats, oaths, honours, and limits. Even Zeus doesn't save his own son.

The Iliad passages

  • 1.396–406 Achilles reminds Thetis how she once saved Zeus when Hera, Poseidon and Athena wanted to chain him: she called the Hundred-Hander Briareus (‘Aegaeon’ to men) and they backed off. Zeus' power once depended on an ally. A trace of an older revolt story.
  • 1.493–611 Thetis supplicates Zeus (knees and chin). Zeus nods, the strongest pledge, and Olympus shakes. Hera quarrels; Zeus threatens her. Hephaestus calms them, recalling how Zeus threw him off Olympus and he fell a whole day to Lemnos. He pours nectar and the gods burst into ‘unquenchable laughter’ at his limping. Feast, Apollo and the Muses sing, bed. Divine conflict ends in comedy; gods can't really suffer.
  • 8.1–27 Zeus forbids interference and proposes the golden chain test: all the gods pulling together couldn't drag him down, but he could haul them all up, with earth and sea too. Raw supremacy.
  • 14.315–328 Seduced by Hera (the ‘Deception of Zeus’), Zeus lists his past lovers (Ixion's wife, Danaë, Europa, Semele, Alcmene, Demeter, Leto) to say he never wanted anyone this much. Comic, and Hera is manipulating the king.
  • 15.185–199 Poseidon refuses Zeus' order: the three brothers drew lots, Zeus the sky, Poseidon the sea, Hades the darkness, with earth and Olympus shared. He claims equal honour. The pantheon as a shared estate (Burkert: parallel with Atrahasis).
  • 16.431–461 Zeus wants to save his son Sarpedon, the Lycian king. Hera objects: if you save yours, the other gods will save theirs. Let Sleep and Death carry the body home to Lycia for a tomb and grave marker, ‘the honour of the dead’. Zeus agrees and weeps bloody rain. Even Zeus accepts fate and the system. (Sarpedon had hero cult in Lycia.)

Ovid, Met. 6.313–381: Latona and the Lycian peasants

  • Latona (Leto), with the newborn Apollo and Diana, arrives thirsty in Lycia. Peasants refuse to let her drink from a pond and stir up the mud. She curses them to live in that pond forever, and they turn into frogs, still croaking insults.
  • Aetiology (frogs), gods punishing disrespect, a Lycian setting. Told as a warning tale in the Niobe episode.

Henrichs, “What Is a Greek God?”

  • Three markers: immortality, anthropomorphism, power.
  • Gods are both persons (with stories, emotions, families) and powers (with cult functions). Not all-powerful, not morally perfect, and they demand honour (timē).

Hamilton and Graves

  • Hamilton's Twelve: Zeus, Poseidon, Hades, Hestia, Hera, Ares, Athena, Apollo, Aphrodite, Hermes, Artemis, Hephaestus (she treats Demeter and Dionysus separately, as earth gods).
  • Graves, “Zeus and Hera”: Zeus courts Hera disguised as a cuckoo; she renews her virginity every year in a spring; the gods rebel and bind Zeus, Thetis brings Briareus; Zeus punishes Hera by hanging her from the sky with golden bracelets and anvils on her ankles.

The pantheon as a system

  • Honours are distributed (timai); each god has a sphere; oaths bind them (by the Styx); the king is strongest but not absolute; fate limits even Zeus.
What they’ll askEssay bait: ‘Is Zeus' power absolute or negotiated?’ For absolute, use 8.1–27. For negotiated, use 1.396–406, 15.185–199, 16.431–461, plus the Hymn to Demeter from Week 7.
W7Death and beyond: Hymns to Demeter and Hermes (+ Tithonus)

Two gods who don't have enough honour (timē) and fight to get it: Demeter by starving the world, Hermes by stealing and charming. Plus Tithonus, the warning about asking the gods for immortality. Taught on Nov 2, one week before the exam. Read it before class.

Homeric Hymn to Demeter

  • Persephone picks flowers with the daughters of Ocean. Gaia grows a narcissus as a trap (by Zeus' plan); Hades seizes her in his chariot. Only Hecate and Helios hear her cry.
  • Demeter searches nine days with torches, no food, no bathing. Helios tells her Zeus gave her daughter to Hades.
  • Furious, she leaves Olympus disguised as an old woman (‘Doso’) and sits by the Maiden's Well at Eleusis. The daughters of King Celeus take her home. Iambe's jokes make her laugh; she refuses wine and drinks the kykeon (barley, water, mint).
  • She nurses baby Demophoon, secretly putting him in the fire to make him immortal. His mother Metaneira sees and screams. Demeter reveals her divinity and demands a temple.
  • She makes the earth produce nothing: humans would die, and the gods would lose their sacrifices. Zeus sends Iris, then all the gods; she refuses. Finally Hermes fetches Persephone, but Hades has given her a pomegranate seed, so she must spend a third of the year below.
  • Demeter restores the grain and teaches her rites, the Eleusinian Mysteries, to the lords of Eleusis (Triptolemus, Eumolpus, Diocles, Celeus). The initiated are blessed; the uninitiated have a different lot after death.
  • Readings: aetiology of the Mysteries and the seasons; a female perspective (a mother-daughter bond vs a marriage arranged between men: Foley); power politics (Demeter forces Zeus to renegotiate honours; Persephone gains honour as queen of the dead); marriage figured as death for the bride.

Homeric Hymn to Hermes

  • Born at dawn in a cave on Mount Cyllene (Arcadia) to Zeus and Maia. By midday he invents the lyre from a tortoise; by evening he steals 50 of Apollo's cattle, drives them backwards, and wears brushwood sandals to hide his tracks.
  • At the Alpheus river he sacrifices two cows and cuts the meat into twelve portions, claiming a place among the gods, but doesn't eat it (he's a god). Then back to the cradle, playing innocent baby.
  • Apollo tracks him (an old man at Onchestus, a bird omen). Hermes lies brilliantly and swears false oaths. On Olympus Zeus laughs and orders the cattle returned.
  • Hermes plays the lyre and Apollo is charmed. The exchange: Apollo gets the lyre (music); Hermes gets the cattle, a golden staff, rule over herds, the job of messenger and guide of the dead, and a lesser kind of prophecy (the bee-maidens).
  • Readings: a newcomer god negotiating honours with cunning (mētis) and exchange instead of force; comedy; the origin of the Apollo–Hermes friendship.

Tithonus (Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite 218–238)

  • Dawn (Eos) asks Zeus to make her Trojan lover Tithonus immortal but forgets to ask for eternal youth. He shrivels; she locks him in a room; his voice babbles on forever. Later versions turn him into a cicada.
  • Lesson: immortality without youth is a curse, and the gap between gods and humans can't be faked. English reception: Tennyson, “Tithonus” (1860), the immortal who begs to die.

Passage drills: identify, then check

  1. A group of goddesses tells a shepherd that they know how to tell many falsehoods that look like truth, and how to speak the truth when they choose.
    Hesiod, Theogony 27–28 (the Muses on Helicon).
    • Poetic authority comes from divine inspiration, but truth is not guaranteed.
    • Hesiod names himself, the first Greek poet to do so (22).
    • Link: Xenophanes and Plato attack poets' lies; Pindar ‘corrects’ myth.
    • Theme: the problem of truth in myth (Bremmer).
  2. Before sea, land and sky, nature had one face: a rough, shapeless heap where cold fought hot, wet fought dry, soft fought hard.
    Ovid, Metamorphoses 1 (opening cosmogony, c. lines 5–20).
    • Chaos as matter, not a gap. Compare Hesiod Th. 116.
    • Philosophical vocabulary (elements in strife).
    • A craftsman god will separate them: order through art, not birth.
    • Sets up the poem's theme of change.
  3. The youngest son took the jagged sickle his mother had made, and from his ambush cut off his father's genitals and threw them behind him.
    Hesiod, Theogony c. 178–182 (castration of Ouranos).
    • Succession myth: violence passes power from father to son.
    • Gaia as plotter: earth vs sky.
    • Results: Erinyes, Giants, Meliae, Aphrodite (aetiologies).
    • Parallel: Kumarbi biting off Anu's genitals. Plato wants this story banned.
  4. You saved him from being bound, when Hera, Poseidon and Pallas Athena planned it: you summoned the hundred-handed one, whom gods call Briareus and men Aegaeon.
    Homer, Iliad 1.396–406 (Achilles to Thetis).
    • Zeus' power was once fragile; he needed an ally.
    • Explains Thetis' leverage when she supplicates Zeus.
    • Trace of an older revolt myth (cf. Graves).
    • Two names, divine and human: a mark of epic language.
  5. Three brothers drew lots: one got the grey sea, one the misty darkness, one the wide sky. Earth and high Olympus belong to all.
    Homer, Iliad 15.187–193 (Poseidon's speech).
    • The pantheon as a shared estate; Poseidon claims equal honour.
    • Contrast with Zeus' claim of supremacy (8.1–27).
    • Apollodorus also has the lot.
    • Burkert: parallel with Atrahasis (Anu, Enlil, Enki).
  6. A thirsty mother with two infants asks peasants for a drink from a pond. They refuse and muddy the water. She curses them to live in the pond forever.
    Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.313–381 (Latona and the Lycian peasants).
    • Aetiology: frogs croak insults.
    • Gods punish disrespect (hybris).
    • Set in Lycia: Leto's cult at the Letoon.
    • Ovid's mix of pathos and humour.
  7. He hid the meat and rich innards in the ox's paunch, and gave Zeus the white bones, artfully wrapped in shining fat.
    Hesiod, Theogony c. 538–541 (Mecone).
    • Aetiology of sacrifice: why gods get the bones.
    • Deception (dolos): ugly outside hides good, pretty outside hides bad (Vernant).
    • Zeus sees through it, which matters for Hesiod's just Zeus.
    • Leads to fire withheld, then the woman.
  8. At first they had eyes but saw in vain, ears but did not hear; they lived like ants in sunless caves, until I showed them the risings and settings of the stars.
    Aeschylus, Prometheus Bound c. 447–458.
    • Prometheus as culture hero: a history of progress.
    • Opposite of Hesiod's decline (Five Ages).
    • Zeus portrayed as tyrant.
    • Reception: Shelley's Prometheus Unbound.
  9. Fearing the human race would be destroyed, the god sent his messenger with respect and justice. ‘Shall I give them to a few, like the arts?’ ‘To all,’ he said.
    Plato, Protagoras 322c–d (Protagoras' myth).
    • Technical skill is not enough; politics needs shared virtue.
    • Justification of democracy: everyone has a share.
    • Contrast with Hesiod's punishing Zeus.
    • A myth used as an argument by a sophist.
  10. He secretly gave her a honey-sweet pomegranate seed to eat, so that she would not stay forever with her mother.
    Homeric Hymn to Demeter (c. 371–374).
    • Food binds her to the underworld: a third of the year below.
    • Marriage as death for the bride.
    • Aetiology of seasons; negotiation of honours.
    • Reception: Milton PL 4.268–272.
  11. On the day he was born, he found a tortoise at the door, laughed, and called it a lucky sign.
    Homeric Hymn to Hermes (c. 24–30).
    • Invention of the lyre: a precocious trickster.
    • Cunning (mētis) as the way to honour.
    • Later swaps the lyre with Apollo for cattle and honours.
    • Comic tone vs Demeter's grief.
  12. Theseus was taking her from Crete to sacred Athens, but had no joy of her: Artemis killed her first on sea-girt Dia, on the word of Dionysus.
    Homer, Odyssey 11.321–325 (Ariadne in the underworld).
    • Earliest version: Ariadne dies.
    • Contrast Th. 947–949 (deified), Ovid (abandoned, crown), Plutarch (many endings).
    • Odd role of Dionysus as accuser.
    • Shows myth as versions.
  13. If oxen, horses and lions had hands and could draw, horses would draw gods like horses, and oxen like oxen.
    Xenophanes, fr. B15.
    • Critique of anthropomorphism.
    • Ionian rationalism (Colophon, Anatolia).
    • Link: Henrichs' anthropomorphism marker; Plato's censorship.
    • With B16: Ethiopian vs Thracian gods.
  14. I cannot call any of the blessed gods a glutton. Some envious neighbour invented the tale that they ate your flesh.
    Pindar, Olympian 1 (c. 46–53).
    • Correcting myth for piety.

Short answers (model answers)

  1. Explain the significance of the Muses' words in Theogony 27–28.
    The Muses say they can tell lies that resemble truth and also tell the truth when they wish. Hesiod uses this to claim divine authority while admitting that poetry can mislead. It anticipates later critics like Xenophanes and Plato, who attack poets for false stories about the gods.
  2. What is the Corycian cave and why does it matter?
    In Apollodorus 1.6.3, Typhon carries the defeated Zeus to the Corycian cave in Cilicia and hides his sinews there in a bearskin guarded by Delphyne. It roots the Typhon myth in Anatolia and connects it with Near Eastern storm-god battles (Mount Casius/Hazzi).
  3. What does Plato object to in Republic 377b–378e?
    He wants the poets' stories censored because they show the gods behaving badly: Ouranos and Kronos, wars among gods, Hera bound by her son. Even if true or allegorical, they will corrupt the young, who can't tell surface from hidden meaning.
  4. Define charter theory with an example from this course.
    Malinowski's charter theory says myths legitimise social institutions and power. Example: Zeus' victory and distribution of honours in the Theogony justifies the Olympian order; Plutarch reports Athenian politicians editing Theseus myths for political advantage.
  5. Why does the pomegranate seed matter in the Hymn to Demeter?
    Eating food in the underworld binds Persephone to it, so she must spend a third of each year with Hades. The compromise explains the seasons and confirms her honour as queen of the dead, while showing that even Demeter's famine can't fully undo Zeus' plan.
  6. What does Herodotus 2.53 tell us about Homer and Hesiod?
    That they lived about 400 years before Herodotus and created the Greeks' theogony, giving the gods their names, honours, skills and forms. It shows Greek religion had no scripture: the poets were the authority.

Essay questions with battle plans

  1. Compare Hesiod's and Ovid's creation accounts. What does each suggest about order and power?
    1. Thesis: Hesiod's cosmos is born through violent family succession and ends in Zeus' political order; Ovid's is crafted out of chaos by a calm intelligence. One is about kingship, the other about change.
    2. Chaos: gap (Th. 116) vs shapeless mass (Met. 1.5–20). Genealogy vs separation.
    3. Violence and power: castration (154–210), swallowing, Titanomachy, ending with Zeus distributing honours (881ff), vs a nameless god calming the elements' quarrel.
    4. Humans: absent from Hesiod's cosmogony (later, with Prometheus) vs the upright climax of Ovid's.
    5. Contexts: Hesiod's Near Eastern background (West, Burkert) vs Ovid's philosophical sources and Augustan Rome (Herodotus 2.53 on Hesiod's authority).
    6. Conclusion: two ideas of what a world is: a kingdom won, or matter shaped. Keats' Hyperion revisits the Titans' fall.
  2. ‘Parallel or influence?’ Discuss Hesiod's succession myth alongside the Hittite and Babylonian texts.
    1. Thesis: some parallels are general (a young storm-god wins), but a few specific details (castrated Sky, swallowing, the stone, Mount Hazzi/Casius) pass Burkert's tests and suggest real influence.
    2. Lay out the pattern: Ouranos/Kronos/Zeus vs Anu/Kumarbi/Teshub vs Apsu/Ea/Marduk.
    3. Specific details: castration and swallowing (Kumarbi); Ullikummi seen from Mt Hazzi = Mt Casius in Apollodorus 1.6.3; Typhon born in Cilicia; Marduk elected king, then ordering the cosmos like Zeus distributing honours.
    4. Route and date: 8th–7th c. BC contacts via Cilicia, North Syria, Phoenicians; Hesiod's father from Cyme in Aeolis.
    5. Caution: differences (Hesiod has no creation from a corpse like Tiamat; Zeus swallows Metis); some features may be independent.
    6. Conclusion: influence for the core, parallel for the general shape. Anatolia as the bridge.
  3. Compare the Prometheus myth in Hesiod, Aeschylus and Plato. How does each version use the story?
    1. Thesis: the same core (trick, fire, punishment) serves three agendas: Hesiod justifies Zeus and the hard human condition; Aeschylus celebrates a rebel benefactor; Plato builds a theory of political justice.
    2. Hesiod (Th. 507–616, W&D): Mecone, fennel stalk, woman as punishment, Pandora's jar; Vernant's reading (gods/humans/beasts, dolos).
    3. Aeschylus (PB 436–506): catalogue of arts, Zeus as tyrant, progress narrative.
    4. Plato (Prot. 320c–322d): Epimetheus, technical skill isn't enough, aidōs and dikē for all.
    5. Why the differences: genre (didactic epic, tragedy, philosophical dialogue) and period (archaic vs 5th–4th c. Athens).
    6. Reception: Shelley's revolutionary Prometheus, Mary Shelley's modern Prometheus.
  4. Is Zeus' power absolute or negotiated?
    1. Thesis: Zeus presents himself as absolute (golden chain) but rules by negotiation, honours and limits.
    2. Absolute: Il. 8.1–27; his nod in Il. 1; Titanomachy and Typhoeus.
    3. Negotiated: he became king by election and distribution of honours (Th. 881ff); Thetis and Briareus saved him (Il. 1.396–406); Poseidon's equal honour (15.185–199).
    4. Limited: Sarpedon (16.431–461), fate and the other gods' claims.
    5. Forced to bargain: Demeter's famine; Hermes' comic trial.
    6. Conclusion: Henrichs' gods are powers with claims; Zeus is a king among stakeholders.
  5. ‘Myths survive as versions, not texts.’ Discuss with Ariadne and one other myth.
    1. Thesis: every source reshapes a myth for its genre, audience and politics, so a myth is the whole set of versions.
    2. Ariadne: Od. 11 (killed), Th. 947–949 (deified), Ovid Met. 8 (abandoned, constellation), Plutarch Thes. 20 (many endings; Peisistratus editing).
    3. Second myth: Prometheus (Hesiod/Aeschylus/Plato) or Pelops (Pindar's correction).
    4. Theory: Bremmer (traditional tales relevant to society); critics who rewrite myth (Xenophanes, Plato, Plutarch).
    5. Conclusion: reception (Chaucer, Shakespeare) continues the same process.
  6. Apply two theories of myth to the Homeric Hymn to Demeter.
    1. Thesis: myth-ritual and charter theory explain different things: the first why the Mysteries exist, the second how honours are allotted in Zeus' order.
    2. Myth and ritual / aetiology: torches, fasting, Iambe's jokes, kykeon, the Eleusinian rites; the seasons.
    3. Charter / political: Demeter forces Zeus to renegotiate; Persephone's honours below; Eleusis' local prestige.
    4. Alternative: structural (life/death, upper/lower world, mother/husband) or Foley's female perspective.
    5. Conclusion: theories are tools; each lights up a different layer.

The background every essay needs in its first paragraph: Reformation, a Protestant queen, a Catholic Spanish enemy, a powerful and fascinating Ottoman Empire, and a brand new commercial theatre industry.

Ideas in the air

  • Humanism: classical learning, human potential, the individual (Erasmus, More). Faustus is a humanist scholar gone wrong.
  • Reformation theology: Calvinist predestination (are some people damned in advance?). Central to Faustus.
  • The Great Chain of Being: a fixed hierarchy from God to stones. Tamburlaine, a shepherd who becomes emperor, smashes it.
  • Machiavelli (The Prince): read in England as a manual of cunning evil. The stage ‘Machiavel’ is born: Lorenzo in Kyd.
  • Seneca: Roman tragedy as the model: five acts, ghosts, revenge, long rhetorical speeches, horror.
  • The Turk: feared (Ottoman expansion, Lepanto, Malta) and admired (wealth, power), and a trading partner. ‘To turn Turk’ meant to convert to Islam, or simply to betray.

The playhouses

  • Open-air amphitheatres (The Theatre, Curtain, Rose, Swan, Globe) in the suburbs and on Bankside, outside the City's control.
  • Thrust stage with the audience on three sides; groundlings standing in the yard for a penny; galleries for richer people. Daylight performances.
  • Little scenery, rich costumes; a trapdoor (hell, graves) and the ‘heavens’ above. All women's roles played by boy actors.
  • Companies protected by noble patrons: the Admiral's Men (Henslowe and the star Edward Alleyn, who played Tamburlaine and Faustus); the Lord Chamberlain's Men (Burbage, Shakespeare).
  • Plays licensed by the Master of the Revels. Puritan enemies of theatre (Stephen Gosson, The School of Abuse, 1579).

Playwrights

  • University Wits: Marlowe, Greene, Peele, Lyly, Nashe, Lodge. University-educated men writing for the commercial stage. Kyd wasn't a university man but worked in the same world.
  • Marlowe's ‘mighty line’ (Ben Jonson's phrase): blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter) made the standard voice of tragedy.
  • Kyd (1558–94): Merchant Taylors' School; arrested in 1593 when ‘heretical’ papers were found, which he said belonged to Marlowe, his former room-mate. Died 1594.
  • Marlowe (1564–93): shoemaker's son from Canterbury, Cambridge scholar, probably a government agent, accused of atheism, killed in Deptford by Ingram Frizer.

W3Shakespeare's Sonnets 18, 60, 130

Two sonnets saying ‘my poetry will beat Time’ (18, 60) and one saying ‘my poetry won't lie like everyone else's’ (130). Know the form, the turn, and two lines from each.

Form and context

  • Shakespearean (English) sonnet: 14 lines of iambic pentameter, three quatrains + a couplet, rhyming ABAB CDCD EFEF GG. The volta (turn) often comes at line 9 and/or in the couplet.
  • Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet: octave + sestet (ABBAABBA CDECDE). Petrarch's lady Laura set the conventions: golden hair, eyes like suns, cruel beauty.
  • Published 1609 by Thomas Thorpe, dedicated to the mysterious ‘Mr. W. H.’ 154 sonnets: 1–126 to the Fair Youth (1–17 urge him to have children), 127–152 to the Dark Lady.

Sonnet 18 “Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?”

  • Q1–Q2: summer is worse than the beloved: too windy (‘rough winds’), too short (‘too short a date’), too hot, and every beauty fades by chance or nature.
  • Volta, line 9: ‘But thy eternal summer shall not fade’. The beloved won't fade because he lives in the poem (‘eternal lines’).
  • Couplet: as long as people breathe and read, ‘this’ (the poem) lives and gives life to you.
  • Themes: poetic immortality, time vs beauty. Devices: extended metaphor, rhetorical question, personified Death bragging, a pun on ‘lines’ (verse lines and lines of time).
  • Irony: the poem ends up praising itself more than the youth.

Sonnet 60 “Like as the waves make towards the pebbled shore”

  • Q1: our minutes rush to their end like waves to a beach, each pushing the one before.
  • Q2: a life from birth (‘Nativity’) to maturity, then ‘crooked eclipses’ attack its glory; Time destroys what it gave.
  • Q3: Time pierces youth's beauty, digs wrinkles in beauty's brow, and mows everything with his scythe.
  • Couplet: yet my verse will stand into the future, praising you despite Time's cruel hand.
  • Note: sonnet 60, about minutes. Devices: simile of waves, personified Time with scythe, astronomical and farming imagery.

Sonnet 130 “My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun”

  • An anti-Petrarchan blazon (a blazon lists the lady's body parts with fancy comparisons). Every comparison is denied: eyes not like the sun, lips less red than coral, breasts dun not snow-white, hair like black wires, no roses in her cheeks, her breath ‘reeks’, her voice is less pleasing than music, and she walks on the ground like a normal woman.
  • Couplet: yet my love is as rare as any woman misrepresented with ‘false compare’.
  • Themes: honest love vs poetic cliché; satire of Petrarchism; the Dark Lady (dark beauty against the fair ideal). Devices: parody, negation, comic plain diction (‘reeks’, ‘wires’).

Connections

  • 18 and 60: art defeats Time. 130: art should tell the truth. 18 makes a grand comparison; 130 mocks comparison itself.
  • Pure Petrarchan hyperbole in Marlowe: Faustus' ‘Was this the face that launched a thousand ships’. Great contrast with 130.

W4Thomas Kyd, The Spanish Tragedy

The play that invented English revenge tragedy. A father who is also a judge can't get justice for his murdered son, goes half mad, and takes revenge through a play-within-a-play. Hamlet is basically its son.

Basics

  • Thomas Kyd; date uncertain (syllabus: 1582; often placed c. 1587); printed 1592. A huge hit, revived and expanded (the 1602 ‘additions’).
  • Setting: the courts of Spain and Portugal after a war Spain has won. Catholic Spain on stage just after the Armada: the audience is primed to see corruption.
  • Frame: the Ghost of Andrea and the figure of Revenge sit on stage and watch the whole play as a chorus.

Who's who

  • Andrea: dead Spanish courtier, killed in battle by Balthazar. His ghost wants revenge.
  • Revenge: allegorical figure; promises Andrea that Bel-imperia will kill Balthazar.
  • Hieronimo: Knight Marshal of Spain, a judge. Father of Horatio. The revenger.
  • Isabella: Hieronimo's wife; goes mad with grief and kills herself.
  • Horatio: their son; captures Balthazar in battle; loves Bel-imperia; murdered.
  • Bel-imperia: Andrea's lover, then Horatio's. Sister of Lorenzo. A strong, active woman who drives the revenge.
  • Lorenzo: her brother; the Machiavel villain who plots everything.
  • Balthazar: Portuguese prince, prisoner in Spain, wants Bel-imperia.
  • Pedringano: Bel-imperia's servant, bribed by Lorenzo. Serberine: Balthazar's servant.
  • King of Spain, Duke of Castile (Lorenzo's father), Viceroy of Portugal (Balthazar's father).

Plot by act

  • Act 1: Andrea's ghost tells his story; Revenge promises satisfaction. Horatio and Lorenzo both claim to have captured Balthazar; the King splits the honour. Bel-imperia turns to Horatio, partly to revenge Andrea.
  • Act 2: Pedringano betrays the lovers' secret meeting. In the arbour, Lorenzo, Balthazar and their servants hang Horatio and stab him. Hieronimo wakes and finds his son's body.
  • Act 3: Bel-imperia's letter written in blood names the killers; Hieronimo suspects a trap. Lorenzo covers his tracks: Pedringano kills Serberine, then is hanged, joking to the end because he thinks a boy's box holds his pardon. It's empty. Pedringano's letter confirms Lorenzo's guilt. Lorenzo blocks Hieronimo from the King. Hieronimo, half mad, weighs Christian patience against revenge in the ‘Vindicta mihi’ soliloquy and chooses secret revenge.
  • Act 4: Isabella cuts down the arbour and kills herself. Hieronimo stages a play for the royal wedding: ‘Soliman and Perseda’, a tragedy at the Turkish court, acted in ‘sundry languages’. The killings are real: Hieronimo kills Lorenzo; Bel-imperia stabs Balthazar, then herself. Hieronimo shows Horatio's corpse, refuses to explain further, bites out his tongue, stabs the Duke of Castile and then himself with a penknife.
  • Epilogue: Andrea's ghost assigns his friends to bliss and his enemies to eternal torments.

Themes

  • Private revenge vs public justice: the judge who can't get justice.
  • Christian ethics vs Senecan revenge: ‘Vengeance is mine, saith the Lord’ (Romans 12:19) against Seneca's ‘the safe way to crime is through more crime’.
  • Madness and grief: Hieronimo and Isabella; parental loss.
  • Court corruption and Machiavellian politics (Lorenzo).
  • Language failing: letters, sundry languages (a Babel), the bitten-out tongue.
  • Metatheatre: a play-within-a-play; Andrea and Revenge as audience; theatre as a weapon.
  • Providence or fate: Revenge ‘sleeps’ in Act 3, but everything still ends as he promised.

The Turk

  • The fatal play-within-a-play is set at the Ottoman court of Suleiman (Soliman), on Rhodes. The Turk becomes the image of tyranny, lust and cruelty, and the stage where Spanish nobles die.
  • Kyd probably later wrote a full play, Soliman and Perseda (c. 1590).

Revenge-tragedy checklist (later in Hamlet)

  • A ghost · a revenger with a just cause but no legal remedy · delay and doubt · madness (real or feigned) · a Machiavel villain · a play-within-a-play or masque · gore · the revenger dies too.
  • Kyd may have written the lost ‘Ur-Hamlet’.

W5Marlowe, Tamburlaine the Great, Part I

A Scythian shepherd talks and fights his way to world empire, cages the Ottoman sultan, slaughters virgins, marries the Egyptian princess, and is never punished. The first great overreacher, in thundering blank verse. (Fun fact: the real Timur beat the real Bayezid I near Ankara in 1402.)

Basics

  • Marlowe, c. 1587, for the Admiral's Men, with Edward Alleyn as Tamburlaine; printed 1590. Part II followed because Part I was a hit.
  • Historical model: Timur (Temür, 1336–1405), who defeated and captured the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I at the Battle of Ankara (1402).
  • The Prologue rejects rhyming clown-comedy and promises high astounding terms: blank verse, epic scale, spectacle.

Who's who

  • Tamburlaine: Scythian shepherd turned conqueror.
  • Zenocrate: daughter of the Soldan of Egypt; captured, falls in love with him.
  • Theridamas: Persian general won over by Tamburlaine's speech. Techelles, Usumcasane: his followers.
  • Mycetes: weak, silly King of Persia. Cosroe: his brother, who uses Tamburlaine and is betrayed.
  • Bajazeth: Emperor of the Turks (the Ottoman sultan). Zabina: his empress.
  • Soldan of Egypt; King of Arabia (Zenocrate's betrothed); Governor of Damascus; Virgins of Damascus; Agydas.

Plot

  • Act 1: Mycetes sends Theridamas against Tamburlaine, who has captured Zenocrate. Tamburlaine wins Theridamas over with words, not swords.
  • Act 2: Cosroe allies with him to depose Mycetes, then Tamburlaine turns on Cosroe (‘ride in triumph through Persepolis’; the ‘aspiring minds’ speech) and becomes King of Persia.
  • Act 3: Bajazeth, besieging Constantinople, insults him. They fight; Zenocrate and Zabina trade insults; Bajazeth is defeated.
  • Act 4: Bajazeth is kept in a cage and used as Tamburlaine's footstool. Siege of Damascus, Zenocrate's father's city: white tents on day one (mercy), red on day two (fighting men die), black on day three (everyone dies).
  • Act 5: the Virgins of Damascus beg for mercy; he has them killed and hung on the walls. Bajazeth and Zabina dash their brains out against the cage. Zenocrate laments. Tamburlaine defeats the Soldan and Arabia (Arabia dies), spares the Soldan for Zenocrate, and crowns her Queen of Persia. Triumph and marriage, no fall.

Themes

  • The overreacher (Harry Levin's term): limitless aspiration, rising above your birth.
  • Fortune's wheel: tragedy normally shows it turning down; Tamburlaine claims to turn it himself.
  • The power of rhetoric: persuasion as conquest.
  • *Machiavellian virtù, cruelty and spectacle; Tamburlaine as the ‘scourge of God’*.
  • The earthly crown as the highest good: shocking (a heretical idea) and unpunished in Part I.
  • Social mobility: a shepherd breaking the Great Chain of Being.

Turks and the East

  • Bajazeth is the Ottoman sultan humiliated on stage. For an audience that feared Ottoman power, the caged sultan is a fantasy of revenge.
  • But it's complicated: Tamburlaine is also Eastern, England was trading with the Ottomans, and ‘the Turk’ works as a mirror for pride and tyranny that the West shares. Bajazeth and Zabina's deaths even get some pathos.

W7Marlowe, Doctor Faustus

A brilliant scholar sells his soul for 24 years of power and spends it on cheap tricks, then has one hour to watch his damnation arrive. Renaissance ambition inside a medieval morality play. Taught Nov 4, a week before the exam. Read it before class.

Basics

  • Marlowe, c. 1588–92 (syllabus: 1592). Two texts: the A-text (1604) and the longer B-text (1616), which has more comedy and is more orthodox.
  • Source: the English Faust Book (1592), from the German Faustbuch (1587).
  • A hybrid: Renaissance tragedy plus a medieval morality play (Good and Evil Angels, the Seven Deadly Sins, the Old Man).

Who's who

  • Faustus: Wittenberg doctor of divinity. Wagner: his servant. Good Angel / Evil Angel.
  • Valdes and Cornelius: magicians who tempt him. Mephistopheles (Mephastophilis), Lucifer, Beelzebub.
  • The Seven Deadly Sins; Robin, Rafe (Dick in the B-text): comic servants who parody conjuring.
  • The Pope; Emperor Charles V; a Knight; the Horse-courser; the Duke and Duchess of Vanholt; the Old Man; Helen of Troy (a spirit); the Scholars.

Plot

  • Prologue: a low-born man rises through learning, ‘swoll'n with cunning’ like Icarus, and the heavens melt his wax wings.
  • 1.1: Faustus rejects logic, medicine, law and divinity. He reads Romans 6:23 (‘the reward of sin is death’) and 1 John 1:8 (‘if we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves’), but cuts off each verse before its promise of grace, concludes we must sin and die (‘Che sarà, sarà’), and turns to necromancy.
  • 1.3: he conjures Mephistopheles, who says he came because Faustus rejected God, not because of the spell: ‘Why, this is hell, nor am I out of it’. Faustus offers his soul for 24 years of service.
  • 2.1: he signs in blood, which congeals; ‘Consummatum est’ (Christ's last words, blasphemously); ‘Homo, fuge’ (‘Man, fly!’) appears on his arm. Devils distract him with a show. He asks for a wife (gets a devil), asks who made the world (refused), wants to repent, and Lucifer distracts him with the Seven Deadly Sins.
  • Act 3: invisible in Rome, he plays tricks on the Pope (snatches food, boxes his ears). Anti-Catholic farce.
  • Act 4: for Emperor Charles V he conjures Alexander the Great; puts horns on a mocking knight; cheats a horse-courser (the horse turns into hay); brings grapes in winter for the Duchess of Vanholt. Meanwhile the servants copy his conjuring.
  • 5.1: scholars ask to see Helen. The Old Man urges repentance; Faustus despairs, Mephistopheles threatens him, and he asks for Helen as his lover: ‘Was this the face…’.
  • 5.2: the final hour. The clock strikes eleven, half past, midnight. He begs time to stop, sees Christ's blood in the sky, wishes to dissolve into mist or water drops. Devils drag him off: ‘I'll burn my books!’
  • Epilogue: ‘Cut is the branch…’, a warning not to practise more than heaven permits.

Themes

  • Knowledge and power: the overreacher again, but turned inward and toward God.
  • Renaissance individualism vs the medieval Christian order.
  • Damnation and predestination: Calvinist debate. Is Faustus a reprobate who can't repent, or a free man who won't? His real sin may be despair.
  • Trivialisation of power: he dreams of empire (1.1, 1.3) and ends up doing magic tricks for dukes.
  • Hell as a state of mind (Mephistopheles' lines).
  • Anti-Catholicism (the Pope scenes) and comedy as parody (servants copying the master).

Tragic shape

  • Rise → fall; hamartia (pride, despair); late anagnorisis in the last soliloquy; catharsis through pity and fear in the final hour.

Shakespeare // Write

Long essay template

  1. Intro (3–4 sentences): context → the key term in the question → your thesis (an argument, not a summary).
  2. Three body paragraphs: point → evidence (short quote or precise scene) → analysis → link back to the question.
  3. Comparing two texts? Compare inside each paragraph.
  4. Conclusion: answer the question, then one wider point.
  5. 40 minutes: 5 plan, 30 write, 5 proofread.

Short-answer template

  1. Define or identify in one sentence.
  2. One specific example from a text.
  3. Why it matters.
  4. Stop at 3–5 sentences.

Short answers (model answers)

  1. What is a ‘Machiavel’? Give an example.
    A stage villain modelled on the English (mis)reading of Machiavelli: a cunning, amoral plotter who enjoys manipulation. Lorenzo in The Spanish Tragedy is one: he arranges Horatio's murder, then removes his accomplices (Serberine, Pedringano) to hide his guilt.
  2. Explain the significance of the ‘Vindicta mihi’ soliloquy.
    In 3.13 Hieronimo quotes Romans 12:19 (‘Vengeance is mine’), the Christian command to leave revenge to God, then switches to Seneca's advice that crime is safest when followed by more crime. The speech dramatises the conflict between Christian patience and private revenge and marks his decision to act in secret.
  3. What is the function of the Prologue in Tamburlaine Part I?
    It rejects rhymed, clownish popular drama (‘jigging veins of rhyming mother-wits’) and promises war and ‘high astounding terms’. It announces Marlowe's new blank-verse heroic drama, and tells the audience to judge Tamburlaine for themselves.
  4. Why is Bajazeth important?
    Bajazeth, the Turkish emperor, is defeated, caged and used as Tamburlaine's footstool, then kills himself with Zabina. He embodies Western fears and fantasies about Ottoman power, but his and Zabina's suffering also gets pathos. Historically, Timur defeated Bayezid I at Ankara in 1402.
  5. Explain ‘Why, this is hell, nor am I out of it’.
    Mephistopheles tells Faustus that hell is not just a place but the condition of being deprived of God. Having seen heaven, he suffers its loss everywhere. It foreshadows Faustus' own fate and shows the devils are honest about damnation while Faustus refuses to listen.
  6. What is the volta in Sonnet 18?
    At line 9 (‘But thy eternal summer shall not fade’) the poem turns from listing summer's faults to the claim that the beloved's beauty is eternal, because it is preserved in the poet's ‘eternal lines’. The couplet completes the turn: the poem gives life to the youth.

Essay questions with battle plans

  1. Compare Tamburlaine and Faustus as ‘overreachers’.
    1. Thesis: both refuse the limits set by birth and God, but Tamburlaine's desire points outward (crowns, conquest) and is rewarded, while Faustus' points upward (knowledge, godhood) and is damned.
    2. Context: Renaissance humanism and social mobility vs the Great Chain of Being; Calvinist theology. Levin's term.
    3. Tamburlaine: ‘aspiring minds’ speech; Fortune's wheel in his hand; rhetoric as conquest; no punishment in Part I.
    4. Faustus: ‘a sound magician is a mighty god’; 24-year pact; trivial use of power; final hour; the Icarus prologue.
    5. Form: heroic spectacle vs morality-play structure (Angels, Sins).
    6. Conclusion: Marlowe tests the limits of human ambition from two sides; the ‘mighty line’ makes both seductive.
  2. Private revenge and public justice in The Spanish Tragedy.
    1. Thesis: Kyd shows a justice system so corrupt that a judge becomes a murderer. Revenge is condemned by Christianity but presented as the only option.
    2. Context: Senecan revenge; English law and the Crown's monopoly on justice; Romans 12:19.
    3. Hieronimo the judge: Lorenzo blocks access to the King; petitioners scene; ‘Vindicta mihi’.
    4. Revenge by theatre: ‘Soliman and Perseda’; real deaths; sundry languages.
    5. Frame: Revenge and Andrea show that a cosmic revenge plot runs above human justice.
    6. Conclusion: ambiguity. Hieronimo achieves justice and becomes a mass murderer. The model for Hamlet.
  3. How are Turks represented in the plays you have studied?
    1. Thesis: the Turk is a figure of tyranny and pride, and a mirror for European desires for power, shaped by fear of and trade with the Ottoman Empire.
    2. Context: Lepanto 1571, Levant Company, Elizabeth and Murad III, ‘turning Turk’.
    3. Tamburlaine: Bajazeth's arrogance, cage, footstool, suicide; the historical Battle of Ankara; pathos in Zabina.
    4. Spanish Tragedy: ‘Soliman and Perseda’ as the stage of killing; the Turkish court as tyranny and lust.
    5. Complication: Tamburlaine is Eastern too; Western courts (Spain) are just as corrupt.
    6. Conclusion: stereotypes that also reflect English anxieties and ambitions.
  4. Is Faustus damned by fate or by choice?
    1. Thesis: the play keeps both readings open. Calvinist predestination vs free will. But it dramatises despair as Faustus' decisive sin.
    2. Fate: misreading of scripture; ‘Che sarà, sarà’; Mephistopheles' threats; the Icarus prologue.
    3. Choice: Good Angel and Old Man keep offering repentance; he repeatedly chooses distraction (Sins, Helen).
    4. Final hour: ‘Christ's blood streams in the firmament’, grace visible but unreachable to a despairing mind.
    5. Conclusion: the tragedy is that he believes he can't repent.
  5. Time and poetic immortality in Sonnets 18 and 60 (with 130 as contrast).
    1. Thesis: 18 and 60 set poetry against Time's destruction; 130 questions the poetic conventions that make such praise possible.
    2. 18: summer's faults; volta at 9; ‘eternal lines’; the couplet's self-praise.
    3. 60: waves/minutes; Nativity to eclipse; Time's scythe; ‘my verse shall stand’.
    4. 130: blazon parodied; ‘false compare’; truth over hyperbole.
    5. Form: quatrain development and couplet resolution.
    6. Conclusion: confidence in art's power, and suspicion of art's lies (contrast Faustus' Helen).
  6. Metatheatre in The Spanish Tragedy and Doctor Faustus.
    1. Thesis: both plays use theatre within theatre to show power: Hieronimo kills through a play, Faustus' magic is a series of shows.
    2. Kyd: Revenge and Andrea as audience; ‘Soliman and Perseda’; ‘Why then I'll fit you’.
    3. Marlowe: devils' dances, the Sins pageant, Alexander, Helen. Illusions that distract from salvation.
    4. Meaning: theatre as both truth-telling and deception; Elizabethan anti-theatrical worries (Gosson).
    5. Conclusion: the Renaissance stage questions its own power.

Victorian // Intel

W1–2The Victorian era: historical and cultural context

1837–1901: Britain becomes the first industrial, urban, imperial superpower, and its literature can't stop worrying about what that costs. Learn the dates and buzzwords; they're your context paragraphs.

Timeline

Keywords

  • The ‘Condition of England’ question (Thomas Carlyle): what has industrialisation done to the poor? Carlyle attacked the ‘cash nexus’, the idea that payment is the only bond between people.
  • Utilitarianism (Bentham, J. S. Mill): the greatest happiness of the greatest number, measured and calculated. Dickens' target in Hard Times.
  • Laissez-faire political economy (Adam Smith, Malthus, Ricardo): the market should run itself; wages follow supply and demand.
  • The Woman Question: women's education, work, legal rights, marriage. The ideal of the ‘Angel in the House’ (Coventry Patmore's poem, 1854) and separate spheres (men public, women domestic). J. S. Mill, The Subjection of Women (1869).
  • Evangelicalism and religious doubt; respectability; the work ethic.
  • Empire: wealth from colonies (Jamaica, India) quietly funds English comfort, as in Jane Eyre.

How Victorian fiction worked

  • Serialisation: novels came out in weekly or monthly parts (Dickens' Household Words and All the Year Round). Hence cliffhangers, big casts, recurring catchphrases.
  • Circulating libraries (Mudie's) and the three-volume novel: moral respectability required.
  • Realism: ordinary people, detailed social settings, moral consequences. Plus melodrama, gothic and sentiment mixed in.
  • The social-problem / ‘Condition of England’ / industrial novel: Gaskell's Mary Barton, Dickens' Hard Times, Disraeli's Sybil.

W3Romanticism vs Realism: Austen, Sense and Sensibility (1811)

Pre-Victorian, but it's the bridge. Two sisters: Elinor (sense: self-control) and Marianne (sensibility: Romantic feeling). Marianne nearly dies of feeling too much. Behind the love plots, it's a novel about money.

Romanticism vs Realism

Plot

  • Mr Dashwood dies; Norland Park goes to his son John Dashwood. John promises to help his stepmother and half-sisters, but his wife Fanny talks him down from £3,000 to practically nothing (the famous chapter 2).
  • Mrs Dashwood, Elinor, Marianne and young Margaret move to Barton Cottage in Devonshire, a gift from cousin Sir John Middleton. Busybody Mrs Jennings is his mother-in-law.
  • Elinor loves Edward Ferrars (Fanny's brother), who is quiet and secretly engaged to the scheming Lucy Steele. Lucy tells Elinor in confidence; Elinor suffers in silence.
  • Marianne twists her ankle in the rain and is rescued by dashing Willoughby, who shares her taste for poetry. They act like an engaged couple (he cuts a lock of her hair), then he leaves suddenly.
  • In London, Willoughby snubs Marianne and marries the rich Miss Grey. We learn he seduced and abandoned Eliza, the ward of the older, steady Colonel Brandon.
  • Marianne falls dangerously ill at Cleveland after walking in wet grass. Willoughby turns up and confesses to Elinor: he did love Marianne but chose money.
  • Edward's engagement comes out; his mother disinherits him; Lucy switches to his brother Robert, who got the money. Edward is free and marries Elinor, taking the parish of Delaford from Brandon. Marianne, wiser, marries Brandon.

Themes

  • Sense vs sensibility: not a simple opposition. Elinor feels deeply but controls it; Marianne learns restraint. The novel argues for a balance.
  • Money and marriage: inheritance through the male line leaves women poor; marriage is women's only economic future. Willoughby and Lucy both pick money over love.
  • Women's dependence: the Dashwood women's fate depends on a brother's kindness and a cousin's charity.
  • Privacy and propriety: secrets (Lucy's engagement, Eliza's story) and the social cost of showing feelings.

Technique

  • Free indirect discourse: the narrator slips into a character's thoughts (mostly Elinor's), so we judge through her.
  • Irony: the money-talk in chapter 2 is pure comic irony.
  • Satire of Romantic sensibility: Marianne's cult of feeling, the picturesque, dead leaves.

W4Gender and the Woman Question: Charlotte Brontë, Jane Eyre (1847)

A poor, plain orphan governess insists she has a soul equal to any man's, refuses to be anyone's mistress, and comes back only when she has money and he has been cut down to size. Meanwhile there's a woman locked in the attic, which critics have been arguing about ever since.

Basics

  • Published 1847 under the male pseudonym ‘Currer Bell’; subtitled An Autobiography. First-person, told by the older Jane looking back.
  • A Bildungsroman (growth story) in five places: Gateshead → Lowood → Thornfield → Moor House → Ferndean. Gothic elements (red-room, attic, fire, mysterious laughter).

Plot by place

  • Gateshead: orphan Jane lives with her cruel aunt Mrs Reed and bullying cousin John Reed. Locked in the red-room where her uncle died, she sees a light, panics, and faints. Sent away to school.
  • Lowood: charity school run by the hypocritical Evangelical Mr Brocklehurst (starves the girls, preaches humility while his family wears silk). Jane's friend Helen Burns preaches Christian endurance and dies of consumption; kind Miss Temple is the teacher. Jane stays eight years (six as pupil, two as teacher).
  • Thornfield: governess to Adèle, ward of Mr Rochester. They meet when his horse slips on ice. Strange laughter (blamed on Grace Poole); Jane saves Rochester from a fire in his bed. He courts the beautiful Blanche Ingram to make Jane jealous; disguises himself as a gypsy fortune-teller; Mason is attacked at night. Rochester proposes under the chestnut tree, which lightning splits that night.
  • The wedding is stopped: Rochester is already married to Bertha Mason, a Creole woman from Jamaica, ‘mad’ and locked in the attic. Rochester asks Jane to live with him anyway. She refuses and flees with nothing.
  • Moor House: starving, she's taken in by St John Rivers and his sisters Diana and Mary, who turn out to be her cousins. She inherits £20,000 from her uncle in Madeira and shares it equally among the four. St John, a cold missionary, proposes a loveless marriage to go to India. On the point of accepting, she hears Rochester's voice calling her name.
  • Ferndean: Thornfield has burned down; Bertha set the fire and jumped from the roof. Rochester is blind and has lost a hand. ‘Reader, I married him.’ He later recovers some sight and sees their first son.

Themes

  • Gender and the Woman Question: Jane demands equal feeling, work and dignity for women (the ch. 12 rooftop speech).
  • Class: the governess is neither servant nor lady. Jane only marries Rochester once she is financially independent and he is humbled: equality before marriage.
  • Passion vs principle: she loves Rochester but won't be his mistress; she respects St John but won't marry without love. She rejects both extremes.
  • Religion: three models: Brocklehurst (hypocrisy), Helen (patient endurance), St John (ambitious self-sacrifice). Jane finds her own.
  • Empire: Bertha from Jamaica, the uncle's money from Madeira, St John's India. Colonial wealth and colonial ‘others’ underwrite the happy ending.

Critics to name

  • *Gilbert & Gubar, The Madwoman in the Attic (1979)*: Bertha is Jane's dark double, the rage a Victorian woman must lock away.
  • *Jean Rhys, Wide Sargasso Sea (1966)*: a postcolonial rewriting that gives Bertha (Antoinette) her own story.
  • Gayatri Spivak (1985): the novel's feminist individualism depends on silencing the colonial woman.

Devices

  • First-person retrospective narration and direct addresses to the reader.
  • Fire and ice imagery (passion vs coldness: St John is ice); the red-room (imprisonment, anger); birds (freedom, captivity); the split chestnut tree (omen).
  • Doubling: Jane / Bertha; Rochester / St John.

W5Class, work, child labour: Dickens, Hard Times (1854) + EBB, “The Cry of the Children” (1843)

Dickens attacks a philosophy (Utilitarian ‘Facts’) that treats children as empty containers and workers as ‘Hands’. Barrett Browning attacks the same system in verse, through children working in mines and mills. Both are protest literature.

Hard Times: basics

  • Serialised weekly in Dickens' magazine Household Words (1854); dedicated to Thomas Carlyle. Short, a ‘moral fable’ (F. R. Leavis).
  • Set in Coketown, a fictional northern mill town (modelled partly on Preston, where there was a big strike in 1853–54).
  • Three books with farming titles: ‘Sowing’, ‘Reaping’, ‘Garnering’. Gradgrind sows Facts and reaps disaster.

Who's who

  • Thomas Gradgrind: retired merchant, MP, Utilitarian; raises his children on Facts only. Changes at the end.
  • Louisa Gradgrind: his daughter; emotionally starved; marries Bounderby out of duty; nearly runs off with Harthouse.
  • Tom Gradgrind (‘the whelp’): selfish son; robs Bounderby's bank and frames Stephen.
  • Josiah Bounderby: mill owner and banker, ‘the Bully of humility’; boasts he was abandoned in a ditch and rose alone. A lie: his loving mother, Mrs Pegler, raised him.
  • Sissy Jupe: circus girl (‘girl number twenty’); can't define a horse; full of kindness. The novel's heart.
  • Bitzer: model pupil; defines a horse perfectly; grows into pure self-interest.
  • Stephen Blackpool: honest power-loom weaver; trapped in marriage to a drunken wife (divorce is only for the rich); loves Rachael; refuses to join the union because of a promise, is shunned by workers and sacked by Bounderby; framed; falls into the Old Hell Shaft and dies. ‘It's aw a muddle.’
  • Slackbridge: loud union agitator (Dickens is suspicious of unions). James Harthouse: bored gentleman who tempts Louisa. Mrs Sparsit: Bounderby's snobbish housekeeper, spying on Louisa (her imagined ‘staircase’). Mr Sleary: lisping circus owner: ‘People mutht be amuthed.’

Plot

  • Gradgrind's school: Sissy can't define a horse; Bitzer can. Sissy's father vanishes; Gradgrind takes her in.
  • Louisa marries Bounderby (30 years older), to help Tom. Stephen asks Bounderby about divorce; refused. He won't join the union, is shunned, then fired.
  • Tom robs the bank and casts suspicion on Stephen, who has left town. Harthouse tempts Louisa; she flees to her father and collapses: his system has left her empty.
  • Rachael and Sissy find Stephen dying in the Old Hell Shaft. Tom is exposed; Bitzer tries to arrest him for advancement; the circus helps Tom escape abroad. Bounderby is exposed by Mrs Pegler and dies later in a fit; Gradgrind reforms.

Themes

  • Fact vs Fancy: Utilitarian education kills imagination, feeling and moral sense. The circus = fancy, play, compassion.
  • Industrial capitalism and class: workers reduced to ‘Hands’ (metonymy); Bounderby's self-made-man myth.
  • Education: children as ‘little vessels’ to be filled with facts.
  • Marriage and divorce law: Stephen's trap; Louisa's loveless marriage.
  • Dickens' limits: sympathetic to workers but distrusts unions (Slackbridge) and offers moral, not political, solutions.

“The Cry of the Children” (Elizabeth Barrett Browning, 1843)

  • Inspired by R. H. Horne's report for the Children's Employment Commission (1842–43) on children in mines and factories.
  • Epigraph from Euripides' Medea: a mother asking her children why they look at her. Children as victims of their own society.
  • The children weep while lambs bleat and birds sing: nature's playtime vs their work. They'd rather die; little Alice is happy in her grave.
  • In the factories, the iron wheels turn all day and the children's heads and hearts turn with them. Machine rhythm in long, heavy lines and repetition.
  • They can't pray: they only know the words ‘Our Father’, and God seems as distant as their masters. Religion has failed them too.
  • Ends by turning on the nation: the child's sob ‘curses deeper’ than a strong man's anger.
  • Devices: direct address (‘O my brothers’), refrain-like repetition, contrast with nature, apostrophe to the ‘cruel nation’, accusatory irony (‘the country of the free’).

Pairing them

  • Both attack a system that treats children as instruments: Gradgrind's classroom and the factory wheel.
  • EBB uses pathos and religious outrage; Dickens uses satire and caricature plus a sentimental heart (Sissy, Stephen).
  • Both appeal to middle-class readers' conscience rather than calling for revolution.

W6Revolution and sacrifice: Dickens, A Tale of Two Cities (1859)

London and Paris, before and during the French Revolution. Dickens hates the cruelty of the aristocrats and is terrified by the cruelty of the mob. A drunken lawyer gives his life for another man and finally means something.

Basics

  • Serialised weekly in All the Year Round (1859). Historical novel; Dickens' main source was *Carlyle's The French Revolution*** (1837).
  • Three books: ‘Recalled to Life’, ‘The Golden Thread’, ‘The Track of a Storm’.
  • Famous opening built on antithesis: ‘It was the best of times, it was the worst of times…’

Who's who

  • Dr Alexandre Manette: unjustly imprisoned 18 years in the Bastille (cell ‘One Hundred and Five, North Tower’); makes shoes in his trauma.
  • Lucie Manette: his daughter, the ‘golden thread’ binding everyone. Marries Darnay.
  • Charles Darnay: French aristocrat (real name Evrémonde) who renounces his family's title and lives in England.
  • Sydney Carton: brilliant, drunk, self-hating English lawyer; looks exactly like Darnay; loves Lucie hopelessly.
  • Mr Stryver: pushy barrister who takes credit for Carton's work. Jarvis Lorry: loyal banker from Tellson's.
  • Ernest and Madame Defarge: wine-shop owners in Saint Antoine, revolutionaries. Madame Defarge knits a secret register of names to be killed; her family was destroyed by the Evrémondes.
  • Marquis St. Evrémonde: Darnay's cruel uncle; runs over a peasant child and tosses a coin; is murdered by the father, Gaspard.
  • Jerry Cruncher: Tellson's messenger and secret ‘resurrection man’ (grave robber). Miss Pross: Lucie's fierce English nurse.

Plot

  • Book 1: 1775. Lorry and Lucie go to Paris to bring Manette, ‘recalled to life’, back to London.
  • Book 2: Darnay is tried for treason at the Old Bailey; Carton saves him by showing how alike they look. Lucie marries Darnay; Carton promises to give his life for her if needed. In France the Marquis is murdered. 1789: the Bastille is stormed; Defarge searches Manette's old cell. Darnay goes to Paris to help his servant Gabelle.
  • Book 3: Darnay is arrested as an emigrant aristocrat; Manette's fame frees him, but he's re-arrested. At the second trial, a letter Manette wrote in the Bastille is read: it curses the Evrémonde family for their crimes. Darnay is condemned.
  • Carton drugs Darnay, swaps places with him in prison, and goes to the guillotine, comforting a young seamstress. Miss Pross fights Madame Defarge, whose pistol goes off and kills her (Pross goes deaf). Carton's last thoughts: a prophecy of peace and the line about a ‘far, far better thing’.

Themes

  • Resurrection: Manette recalled to life, Darnay saved from death twice, Carton's spiritual rebirth through sacrifice; Cruncher's grave-robbing parodies it.
  • Sacrifice and redemption: Carton.
  • Revolution and violence: sympathy for the oppressed (the Marquis, starving Saint Antoine) and horror at the Terror (La Guillotine, Madame Defarge). Oppression breeds violence: a warning to England about its own poor.
  • Doubles: Carton/Darnay; London/Paris; Lucie/Madame Defarge.
  • Imprisonment: the Bastille, Manette's mind, Carton's wasted life.
  • Fate and history: the past (Manette's letter) returns to condemn the present.

Devices

  • Antithesis and parallelism (the opening). Symbols: knitting, spilled wine in the street (foreshadowing blood), shoemaking, the golden thread, the ‘sea’ of the mob. Foreshadowing and coincidence (serial form).

W7Industrial fiction: Gaskell, Mary Barton (1848) + Engels, The Condition of the Working Class (1845)

Manchester in the hungry 1840s, seen twice: by a minister's wife who wants rich and poor to understand each other (Gaskell), and by a German factory-owner's son who says the system is killing people on purpose (Engels). Taught Nov 6, a week before the exam.

Mary Barton: basics

  • Published anonymously in 1848; subtitle A Tale of Manchester Life. Elizabeth Gaskell was the wife of a Unitarian minister in Manchester and worked among the poor.
  • In her Preface she says she knows nothing of political economy; she writes to give voice to the suffering ‘dumb people’ and to help the classes understand each other.
  • A ‘Condition of England’ / industrial novel. Originally titled John Barton: he's the tragic centre.

Who's who

  • John Barton: mill worker, union man and Chartist. Loses his wife; his son died during unemployment while the mill owners stayed comfortable. Embittered, he takes opium.
  • Mary Barton: his daughter, a dressmaker. Flattered by Harry Carson, but really loves Jem.
  • Jem Wilson: honest engineer who loves Mary.
  • Harry Carson: rich mill owner's son; wants Mary as a mistress; mocks the workers.
  • Mr Carson: his father, mill owner.
  • Esther: Mary's aunt, a ‘fallen woman’ (prostitute) who tries to warn Mary.
  • Job Legh (self-taught naturalist) and his blind granddaughter Margaret; Will Wilson (sailor, Jem's cousin); the Davenports (a family dying of fever in a cellar).

Plot

  • 1839: the Chartist petition is rejected by Parliament; Barton returns from London bitter. The Davenport cellar scene shows starvation and typhus.
  • Mary rejects Jem, hoping to marry Harry Carson and lift her father out of poverty, then realises she loves Jem.
  • During a strike, Harry Carson draws a cruel caricature of the workers' delegation. The union men draw lots to kill him; John Barton draws the lot and shoots him.
  • Jem's gun is found at the scene: Jem is arrested. But the gun's paper wadding is a torn piece of a Valentine with Mary's name on it, which her father had been using to copy a poem. Mary finds the matching piece at home: she knows the truth.
  • To save Jem without betraying her father, Mary chases Will Wilson (Jem's alibi) down the Mersey by boat. Jem is acquitted at the Liverpool trial; Mary collapses.
  • Dying, John Barton confesses to Mr Carson, who at first wants revenge, then (reading the Gospel) forgives him. Barton dies in Carson's arms. Carson starts improving conditions. Mary and Jem emigrate to Canada.

Themes

  • Class conflict and the gulf between masters and men: misunderstanding and indifference, not pure evil.
  • Christian reconciliation: forgiveness and sympathy as the cure (Carson and Barton).
  • Poverty, disease, hunger: realism about Manchester slums.
  • Women's work and sexual danger: Mary as dressmaker, Esther as warning.
  • Limits: the ending sends the young couple abroad. Gaskell can imagine sympathy but not a changed system. Critics like W. R. Greg said she was unfair to the masters.

Engels, The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845)

  • Written in German by Friedrich Engels (24), who was working in his family's cotton firm in Manchester; English translation 1887. Guided round the slums by the Irish worker Mary Burns.
  • Describes the Old Town, the river Irk, ‘Little Ireland’: filth, overcrowding, cellars, disease.
  • Shows how Manchester's layout hides the poor: the main streets are lined with shops, so the middle class can commute through the city without ever seeing a working-class district.
  • Accuses the bourgeoisie of ‘social murder’: society knowingly keeps workers in conditions that kill them early.
  • Predicts that class war and revolution are inevitable. A founding text of Marxism (with Marx he wrote the Communist Manifesto in 1848).

Short answers (model answers)

  1. What is the ‘Condition of England’ novel? Give an example.
    A Victorian novel addressing the social effects of industrialisation, especially on the poor, a term from Carlyle's ‘Condition of England question’. Examples: Gaskell's Mary Barton (1848) and Dickens' Hard Times (1854). They use realism and sympathy to make middle-class readers face working-class suffering.
  2. Explain the significance of Bertha Mason.
    Rochester's Creole wife from Jamaica, locked in Thornfield's attic, is the obstacle to Jane's marriage and her dark double: Gilbert and Gubar read her as Jane's repressed rage. Postcolonial critics (Rhys, Spivak) see her as the colonial woman whose silencing makes Jane's happy ending possible.
  3. Who is Bounderby and what does he represent?
    Josiah Bounderby is Coketown's mill owner and banker, the ‘Bully of humility’ who boasts of being a self-made man abandoned as a child. The lie is exposed by his mother, Mrs Pegler. He represents the hypocrisy of the self-made-man myth and capitalist contempt for workers.
  4. Why is ‘Recalled to Life’ important in A Tale of Two Cities?
    It is Lorry's coded message about Manette's release and the title of Book 1. It introduces the resurrection theme: Manette restored after 18 years in the Bastille, Darnay saved from death, Cruncher's grave-robbing as parody, and Carton's spiritual rebirth through his final sacrifice.
  5. How does Engels' text relate to Mary Barton?
    Both describe 1840s Manchester: cellars, fever, hunger. Engels complements Gaskell's scenes with analysis, but contradicts her solution: Gaskell hopes for Christian sympathy between masters and men, while Engels calls the system ‘social murder’ and predicts class war.
  6. What does Sissy Jupe represent?
    The circus girl ‘girl number twenty’ can't give a factual definition of a horse but has compassion, loyalty and imagination. She represents Fancy against Gradgrind's Fact, and quietly heals the Gradgrind family that the system damaged.

Essay questions with battle plans

  1. How does Jane Eyre engage with the Victorian ‘Woman Question’?
    1. Thesis: Brontë demands spiritual and emotional equality for women, but grounds the happy ending in money and in silencing Bertha. Radical and limited at once.
    2. Context: separate spheres, ‘Angel in the House’, the governess's ambiguous status; published 1847 as ‘Currer Bell’.
    3. Equality: ch. 12 (‘women feel just as men feel’), ch. 23 (‘poor, obscure, plain, and little’; ‘I am no bird’).
    4. Independence: refuses to be Rochester's mistress (ch. 27), refuses St John; returns with £20,000 to a humbled Rochester. ‘Reader, I married him.’
    5. Complication: Bertha (Gilbert & Gubar; Rhys; Spivak) and colonial wealth.
    6. Devices: first-person retrospective voice, bird and fire/ice imagery.
    7. Conclusion: a feminist voice built on others' silence.
  2. ‘Fact’ and ‘Fancy’ in Hard Times.
    1. Thesis: Dickens shows that Utilitarian ‘Fact’ produces emotionally dead, selfish people, and that ‘Fancy’ (imagination, play, compassion) is essential to humanity.
    2. Context: Utilitarianism, political economy, the Preston strike, Carlyle.
    3. Fact: Gradgrind's school, Bitzer's horse, Louisa's marriage by calculation, Bitzer's self-interest.
    4. Fancy: Sissy, the circus, Sleary's ‘People mutht be amuthed’.
    5. Collapse: Louisa's breakdown (‘conscious death’), Tom's crime, Gradgrind's conversion.
    6. Devices: caricature, satire, symbolic names, ‘Hands’, the Sowing/Reaping/Garnering structure.
    7. Conclusion: a moral rather than political solution.
  3. Compare the representation of industrial workers in Hard Times and Mary Barton (with Engels).
    1. Thesis: both novels humanise workers for middle-class readers, but Gaskell gives them political grievances and anger while Dickens gives them patience and suffering. Engels shows what both soften.
    2. Dickens: Stephen Blackpool (honest, passive, ‘a muddle’), ‘Hands’, Slackbridge, distrust of unions.
    3. Gaskell: John Barton (Chartist, bitter, a murderer), Davenport cellar, the lottery of murder, Christian reconciliation.
    4. Engels: hidden slums, ‘social murder’, class war; complicates both novels' sympathy.
    5. Endings: Stephen dies; Barton dies forgiven; Mary and Jem emigrate. No structural change.
    6. Conclusion: the industrial novel's power and its limits.
  4. The individual in times of revolution: A Tale of Two Cities.
    1. Thesis: Dickens sympathises with the oppressed but fears the mob; the novel answers political violence with individual sacrifice.
    2. Context: Carlyle's French Revolution; English fears of revolution (Chartism, 1848).
    3. Oppression breeds violence: the Marquis and the child, Saint Antoine, the spilled wine.
    4. The mob: La Guillotine, Madame Defarge's knitting and revenge.
    5. Individuals: Manette (recalled to life, his letter returns to destroy), Darnay (renounces his name), Carton (resurrection through sacrifice).
    6. Devices: antithesis, doubles, resurrection motif.
    7. Conclusion: a warning to England, and a Christian answer.
  5. How do Hard Times and “The Cry of the Children” protest against the treatment of children?
    1. Thesis: both show children turned into instruments of a system, Dickens through satire, Barrett Browning through pathos and religious outrage.
    2. Context: Factory Acts, Mines Act 1842, Horne's report.
    3. Dickens: ‘little vessels’, girl number twenty, Louisa and Tom ruined by education.
    4. EBB: weeping children vs lambs and birds; iron wheels; lost faith; ‘O cruel nation’.
    5. Form: novel's caricature vs poem's direct address and rhythm.
    6. Conclusion: appeals to conscience, not revolution.
  6. Romanticism vs Realism: what does Sense and Sensibility teach about feeling?
    1. Thesis: Austen critiques Romantic sensibility through Marianne's near-fatal excess, but doesn't reject feeling. Elinor's controlled feeling is the realist ideal.
    2. Romantic traits: Marianne's poetry, the picturesque, Willoughby as a Romantic hero.
    3. Realism: money in ch. 2, inheritance, social consequences, women's dependence.
    4. Devices: irony, free indirect discourse.
    5. Link to the Victorians: inheritance and marriage lead into the Woman Question (Jane Eyre).

Drama // Intel

IDrama studies toolkit

The vocabulary you'll need to talk about any play without sounding like you're summarising a film.

Aristotle, Poetics (classical basics)

  • Mimesis: drama as imitation of action.
  • Six elements: plot, character, thought, diction, song, spectacle (plot most important).
  • Hamartia (tragic error), peripeteia (reversal), anagnorisis (recognition), catharsis (purging of pity and fear).
  • The three unities (time, place, action): Aristotle stressed unity of action; neoclassical critics added time and place.

Structure and devices

  • Freytag's pyramid: exposition → rising action → climax → falling action → dénouement.
  • Dramatic irony: the audience knows what a character doesn't.
  • Soliloquy (alone, thinking aloud), aside (to the audience), monologue (long speech to others).
  • Stage directions: the playwright's instructions; in modern drama they carry huge meaning (Beckett's ‘They do not move’).
  • Subtext: what is meant but not said (Pinter).

Theatres and styles

  • Proscenium arch and the fourth wall: the realist stage as a room with one wall removed.
  • The well-made play (Scribe, Sardou): tight plot, secrets revealed, neat resolution. What 1950s writers rebelled against.
  • Realism (Ibsen) and naturalism (Zola, Strindberg): ordinary life, heredity and environment shape people.
  • Epic theatre (Brecht): breaks illusion with the alienation effect so the audience thinks critically. (Week X, but useful as a contrast.)
  • Text vs performance: a play is a script for a live event; always think about how a scene looks and sounds on stage.
What they’ll askUse these terms in every answer. ‘The peripeteia comes when…’ or ‘Pinter withholds exposition’ sounds like analysis; plot summary doesn't.
IIComedy of Manners and Sentimental Drama

Two opposite kinds of comedy. Comedy of manners laughs at upper-class sex, money and hypocrisy with sharp wit. Sentimental drama wants you to cry and become a better person. They're the long background to modern British stage comedy.

Comedy of manners

  • Restoration (after 1660): Charles II reopens the theatres the Puritans closed in 1642; actresses appear on the English stage for the first time.
  • Plays: Etherege, The Man of Mode (1676); Wycherley, The Country Wife (1675); *Congreve, The Way of the World*** (1700).
  • Features: witty dialogue (repartee); fashionable London society; intrigue, adultery, the marriage market; stock types: the rake/libertine, the fop, the witty heroine, the jealous husband, the country fool. Satire of manners, not morals.
  • Revival in the 1770s, ‘laughing comedy’: Goldsmith, She Stoops to Conquer (1773); Sheridan, The Rivals (1775) and The School for Scandal (1777).
  • Victorian peak: *Wilde, The Importance of Being Earnest*** (1895).

Sentimental drama

  • Reaction against Restoration ‘immorality’, especially after Jeremy Collier's attack, A Short View of the Immorality and Profaneness of the English Stage (1698).
  • Plays: Colley Cibber, Love's Last Shift (1696), where the rake reforms in Act 5; *Richard Steele, The Conscious Lovers*** (1722).
  • Features: virtuous middle-class characters; moral lessons; distressed heroines; reformed rakes; pathos and tears (‘weeping comedy’); happy endings that reward virtue.
  • Related: domestic/bourgeois tragedy, e.g. George Lillo, The London Merchant (1731).
  • Goldsmith attacked it in ‘A Comparison between Laughing and Sentimental Comedy’ (1773): comedy should make us laugh at folly, not weep at virtue.

IIIVictorian drama, the Irish Literary Revival, and Synge's Riders to the Sea (1904)

Victorian theatre was popular, spectacular and censored; by the 1890s Ibsen, Shaw and Wilde were pushing it toward serious drama. In Ireland a nationalist cultural movement built its own theatre. Synge's one-act tragedy is about an old woman on an island who has lost every man in her family to the sea.

Victorian drama

  • Theatres Regulation Act 1843: ends the monopoly of the patent theatres (Drury Lane, Covent Garden) on spoken drama. Censorship by the Lord Chamberlain continues (from 1737 until 1968).
  • Popular forms: melodrama (villain, virtuous heroine, music, sensation, spectacle: Jerrold's Black-Eyed Susan, Boucicault), farce, burlesque, pantomime, music hall.
  • Reform: T. W. Robertson's ‘cup-and-saucer’ realism (Caste, 1867); Gilbert & Sullivan; actor-managers like Henry Irving.
  • 1890s ‘New Drama’: Ibsen's influence (A Doll's House in London, 1889); problem plays by Pinero (The Second Mrs Tanqueray, 1893) and Shaw (Mrs Warren's Profession, banned); Wilde's comedies.

Irish Literary Revival

  • Late 19th–early 20th c. cultural nationalism after the fall of Parnell (1891). Douglas Hyde's Gaelic League (1893) revives Irish language and culture.
  • W. B. Yeats, Lady Gregory and Edward Martyn found the Irish Literary Theatre (1899); it leads to the Abbey Theatre (1904).
  • Aims: Irish subjects, folklore, peasant life, Celtic myth, Hiberno-English speech; against the comic ‘stage Irishman’.
  • Synge's The Playboy of the Western World caused riots at the Abbey (1907): nationalists felt it insulted Irish peasants.

Riders to the Sea: the play

  • One act; first performed 1904 by the Irish National Theatre Society. John Millington Synge spent summers on the Aran Islands (1898–1902) and wrote The Aran Islands (1907).
  • Setting: a cottage kitchen on an island off the west of Ireland. Nets, oilskins, a spinning wheel, new white boards for a coffin.
  • Characters: Maurya (old mother), her daughters Cathleen and Nora, her last son Bartley; women keeners and men who carry the body.
  • Maurya has lost her husband, her husband's father, and five sons to the sea. Michael is missing. A young priest has sent clothes from a drowned man found in Donegal.
  • Bartley insists on taking horses to the Galway fair by sea. Maurya begs him to stay; he goes; she fails to give him her blessing. The girls send her after him with bread.
  • Nora and Cathleen identify Michael's clothes by the stitches Nora knitted in his stocking.
  • Maurya returns: at the spring well she saw Bartley on the red mare and Michael, dead, riding the grey pony behind him, in fine clothes. She couldn't speak. She knows what it means.
  • Men bring in Bartley's body: the grey pony knocked him into the sea. Maurya sprinkles holy water, calm at last: the sea can't hurt her any more. They have the boards for the coffin but forgot the nails.

Themes and readings

  • Fate and nature: the sea as an unbeatable antagonist; the vision as omen.
  • Pagan vs Christian: holy water and the priest's promise that God won't leave her destitute (bitter irony) beside the vision, the keening, folk belief.
  • Women's experience: the women wait, wash, knit and mourn; the men go out and die.
  • Poverty: the cost of rope and boards, the need to sell horses; survival forces men onto the sea.
  • Tragedy: often compared to Greek tragedy. Maurya as a tragic figure; compressed into one act; acceptance replaces catharsis.
  • Language: lyrical Hiberno-English rhythms. The Revival's ideal of authentic peasant speech.

Ammo: lines to quote

They're all gone now, and there isn't anything more the sea can do to me.
Maurya · Grief becomes release.
No man at all can be living for ever, and we must be satisfied.

What they’ll askLikely question: ‘How does Riders to the Sea reflect the aims of the Irish Literary Revival?’ Peasant life, Aran, Hiberno-English, folklore, dignity rather than stage-Irish comedy, tragedy built from local life.
IVPost-war drama, Existentialism and the Absurd: Beckett, Waiting for Godot (1953)

After the Second World War, the Holocaust and the atom bomb, a play where two tramps wait for someone who never comes, twice. Critic Vivian Mercier's famous summary: ‘a play in which nothing happens, twice.’

Context and philosophy

  • Post-war Europe: ruins, the Holocaust, Hiroshima, the Cold War. Old certainties (God, progress, reason) collapse.
  • Existentialism (Sartre): ‘existence precedes essence’: no fixed human nature, we create ourselves through choices; freedom and responsibility; bad faith = pretending we aren't free.
  • *Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus (1942): the absurd* is the clash between our need for meaning and the silent, irrational world. Sisyphus pushing his rock forever.
  • Theatre of the Absurd: term from Martin Esslin's book (1961). Beckett, Ionesco (The Bald Soprano), Genet, Adamov, early Pinter.
  • Absurd features: no logical plot, circular structure, minimal setting, characters without background, language breaking down (repetition, clichés, nonsense), long silences, comedy mixed with despair. Unlike Sartre and Camus, the Absurdists show meaninglessness in the form itself, not through rational argument.

The play

  • Written in French (En attendant Godot); premiered Paris 1953 (Théâtre de Babylone, dir. Roger Blin); English version London 1955 (Arts Theatre, dir. Peter Hall). Subtitle: ‘a tragicomedy in two acts’. Beckett: Irish, lived in France, in the Resistance during the war; Nobel 1969.
  • Set: a country road, a tree, evening.
  • Vladimir (Didi) and Estragon (Gogo): two tramps waiting for Godot. Didi thinks and worries; Gogo sleeps, eats, suffers with his boots.
  • Pozzo and Lucky: master and slave, linked by a rope. Lucky carries bags, dances, and when ordered to ‘think’ pours out a long nonsense speech of academic fragments.
  • The Boy: Godot's messenger. Each evening: Godot won't come today, but surely tomorrow.
  • Act I: boots, hats, the two thieves crucified with Christ, talk of hanging themselves from the tree; Pozzo and Lucky pass; the Boy comes.
  • Act II: the next day, same place. The tree has a few leaves. Estragon barely remembers yesterday. Pozzo is now blind, Lucky dumb; they fall and can't get up. The Boy comes again and doesn't remember them. They try to hang themselves with Estragon's belt: it breaks, his trousers fall.
  • Both acts end the same way: they agree to go, and do not move.

Themes

  • Waiting and hope: waiting gives life shape but may be pointless.
  • Time and repetition: the two acts mirror each other; memory fails; time is unreliable.
  • God / meaning: Godot as God? Beckett refused to say. The Christian references (two thieves, the Boy who ‘minds the goats’) stay ambiguous.
  • Dependence and friendship: Didi and Gogo can't separate; Pozzo and Lucky are bound by power.
  • Power: Pozzo's tyranny reversed into helplessness.
  • Language: chatter to pass time; Lucky's speech as thought collapsing.
  • Tragicomedy: music-hall and silent-film clowning (bowler hats, falling trousers, Chaplin, Laurel and Hardy) inside a metaphysical despair.

Ammo: lines to quote

Nothing to be done.
Estragon, opening line · Repeated motif: paralysis and the human condition.
a play in which nothing happens, twice
Vivian Mercier, critic · The circular two-act structure.
What they’ll askTypical: ‘Discuss Waiting for Godot as a Theatre of the Absurd play.’ Define the Absurd (Camus, Esslin), then go through form (circular, no exposition), language (repetition, Lucky), stage image (tree, road) and themes (waiting, God).
VThe Angry Young Men: Osborne, Look Back in Anger (1956)

The night British theatre supposedly changed: 8 May 1956, an ironing board on stage and a furious young man shouting at his upper-class wife. The form was quite old-fashioned. The voice was new.

The movement

  • ‘Angry Young Men’: label for 1950s writers furious with the class system, the Establishment, post-war dullness and lost causes. The Royal Court's press officer used it for Osborne, and the press ran with it.
  • Writers: John Osborne, Kingsley Amis (Lucky Jim, 1954), John Wain, John Braine (Room at the Top, 1957), Alan Sillitoe (Saturday Night and Sunday Morning, 1958), Colin Wilson.
  • Context: the welfare state and grammar schools let working-class boys into university but not into the class above; Britain losing its empire (India 1947, Suez Crisis, late 1956); the H-bomb; boredom.
  • Theatre: the English Stage Company at the Royal Court (George Devine), a writers' theatre. Against the polite ‘drawing-room’ plays of Rattigan and Coward. Critic Kenneth Tynan championed the play.

The play

  • Premiered 8 May 1956, Royal Court, directed by Tony Richardson. Three acts. A one-room attic flat in a Midlands town; Sunday papers, an ironing board.
  • Jimmy Porter: early 20s, working-class, university-educated (‘white-tile’), runs a sweet stall. Witty, cruel, funny, self-pitying.
  • Alison Porter: his wife, from an upper-middle-class family; quiet, worn down. Cliff Lewis: their gentle Welsh friend and lodger. Helena Charles: Alison's actress friend, conventional, religious. Colonel Redfern: Alison's father, ex-Indian Army, an Edwardian left behind.
  • Act I: Jimmy rants at the papers, the church, Alison's family; horseplay with Cliff knocks Alison into the ironing board and burns her arm. Alison tells Cliff she's pregnant but hasn't told Jimmy. The ‘bears and squirrels’ game: their private refuge.
  • Act II: Helena arrives and gets Alison to leave; the Colonel comes to fetch her. Jimmy returns; Helena slaps him, then kisses him.
  • Act III: months later Helena is at the ironing board. Cliff leaves. Alison returns: she has lost the baby. Helena goes. Jimmy and Alison reconcile in the bears-and-squirrels game.

Themes

  • Class anger: Jimmy vs Alison's family; education without belonging.
  • Post-imperial nostalgia: the Colonel's lost Edwardian India vs Jimmy's lack of ‘good, brave causes’.
  • Masculinity and misogyny: Jimmy's attacks on women; Alison's suffering as the price of reconciliation. Modern readers find this very uncomfortable.
  • Emotional honesty vs English reticence: Jimmy wants people to feel.
  • Repetition: the ironing board and Sunday papers return with a different woman; nothing really changes.

Form: revolutionary or not?

  • Conventional: three acts, a single realist set, a well-made structure.
  • New: a working-class, provincial anti-hero, long angry monologues, domestic squalor on stage (the start of kitchen-sink realism), young audiences.

Ammo: lines to quote

no good, brave causes left

VIKitchen Sink drama: Arnold Wesker, The Kitchen (written 1957)

Thirty people of different nationalities cook for a restaurant all day. The work speeds up until a young German cook snaps and smashes the place. The owner can't understand why: he pays wages, they eat, what more could they want?

Kitchen Sink drama

  • Realism about working-class life: cramped homes, regional speech, domestic and social problems. The name comes from 1950s paintings (John Bratby) that showed ordinary kitchens.
  • Plays: Osborne's Look Back in Anger, Shelagh Delaney's A Taste of Honey (1958), Wesker's Trilogy (Chicken Soup with Barley, Roots, I'm Talking About Jerusalem). Parallel: British New Wave cinema.
  • Where Osborne gives one angry voice, Wesker shows a whole working community, and the system that shapes it.

The play

  • Written 1957; first staged at the Royal Court in 1959 (a Sunday-night performance, dir. John Dexter); expanded version 1961. Wesker had worked as a pastry cook: the world of the play is his own.
  • Setting: the huge kitchen of the Tivoli, a big London restaurant serving huge numbers of meals every day. Ovens, stations, waitresses rushing in and out. The food is mimed.
  • Structure: Part One: morning, building up to the lunch rush, staged as rhythm and noise. Interlude: the afternoon break, when the workers talk about their lives and dreams. Part Two: evening service and the breakdown.
  • Peter: young German cook, loud, laughing, volatile; in love with Monique, a married waitress who won't leave her husband.
  • Others: Paul, the Jewish pastry cook, and his disappointment with working-class solidarity; Dimitri, a Cypriot porter who builds radios and talks about the joy of making a whole thing instead of little parts; Kevin, the new Irish cook, overwhelmed by the pace; cooks and waitresses of many nationalities, with ethnic tensions.
  • Mr Marango: the old owner, walking around, never understanding.
  • Climax: after quarrels and pressure, Peter smashes the gas supply with a chopper, the ovens die, he wrecks plates and injures his hands. Marango is bewildered: he gives them work and wages and food. What more is there?

Themes

  • Alienated labour (Marx): workers do fragmented tasks, have no control, and become parts of a machine.
  • The kitchen as the world: Wesker said, in effect, that for Shakespeare the world was a stage, but for him it was a kitchen. A microcosm of capitalist society.
  • The poverty of dreams: in the Interlude the workers can barely imagine anything better. That is the real tragedy.
  • Internationalism and prejudice: post-war migrant labour; nationalities clash under pressure.
  • Love impossible under these conditions (Peter and Monique).
  • Form: a huge cast, no single hero, rhythm and choreography instead of plot. Ensemble realism.

VIIComedy of Menace: Harold Pinter, The Birthday Party (1958)

A man is hiding in a shabby seaside boarding house. Two strangers arrive, throw him a ‘birthday party’ he says isn't his birthday, interrogate him into gibberish, and take him away in a black car. Nobody ever explains who they are. It's very funny and deeply unsettling. Taught Nov 6.

Comedy of Menace

  • Term coined by critic Irving Wardle (1958), borrowing a subtitle from David Campton's The Lunatic View: A Comedy of Menace.
  • Features: an enclosed room as a fragile refuge; intruders bringing an unexplained threat; withheld exposition (no clear past or motives); ordinary, comic, everyday talk that turns threatening; power games through language; pauses and silences (‘Pinteresque’).
  • Related to the Absurd, but more realistic in setting and speech. The menace is social and political, not just metaphysical.

The play

  • First performed 1958 at the Lyric Theatre, Hammersmith. Panned and closed within a week; only Harold Hobson (Sunday Times) praised it. Now a classic. Pinter won the Nobel Prize in 2005.
  • Setting: the living room of a run-down seaside boarding house.
  • Meg Boles: in her sixties, motherly, childlike, flirty with Stanley. Petey Boles: her husband, a deckchair attendant, quiet.
  • Stanley Webber: their only lodger, late thirties, unshaven, claims he was a concert pianist. Hiding from something.
  • Goldberg: smooth-talking, Jewish, full of clichés about family and respectability. McCann: Irish, nervous, violent, tears newspaper into strips. Lulu: a young neighbour.
  • Act I: breakfast (cornflakes, fried bread). Meg says two men are coming; Stanley panics. Goldberg and McCann arrive. Meg says it's Stanley's birthday (he denies it) and gives him a toy drum; he beats it wildly.
  • Act II: Goldberg and McCann interrogate Stanley with a barrage of accusations and absurd questions (from betraying ‘the organisation’ to riddles about chickens and eggs) until he screams. The party: toasts, Lulu flirts with Goldberg; a game of blind man's buff; McCann breaks Stanley's glasses; in the blackout Stanley tries to strangle Meg and is found giggling over Lulu on the table.
  • Act III: next morning. Stanley is brought down clean-shaven, in a dark suit and collar, speechless, making only noises. Goldberg and McCann promise him a new, successful life and take him to ‘Monty’ in a big black car. Petey's weak protest fails. Meg, who slept through it, happily recalls being the belle of the ball.

Themes

  • Menace and power: who are they? Gangsters, agents, an organisation, death? The threat is real because it's never explained.
  • Conformity: Stanley, the drop-out artist, is ‘reformed’ into a silent, respectable man. Goldberg and McCann can be read as family/tradition and religion (Jewish and Irish Catholic) enforcing social norms.
  • Identity and the past: nobody's story can be verified (Goldberg's many names, Stanley's career).
  • Language as a weapon: the interrogation; clichés; repetition; small talk that controls.
  • The room: home as false safety; the outside world gets in.
  • Comedy: Meg's banal chatter, the party, the absurd questions. We laugh, then we stop.

Ammo: lines to quote

Stan, don't let them tell you what to do!
Petey, Act III · The only (and useless) resistance.
What they’ll ask‘How does Pinter create menace?’ Withheld exposition, intruders in a room, the interrogation, the stage images (the toy drum, the broken glasses, the transformed Stanley), the pauses. Then ask what the menace stands for.
VIIITheatre of Cruelty: Edward Bond, Saved (1965)

The play with the scene where young men stone a baby to death in a London park. It was banned, prosecuted, and helped end stage censorship in Britain. Bond's point: violence isn't monstrous, it's what a deprived, brutal society produces. Taught Nov 13.

Theatre of Cruelty

  • Antonin Artaud, The Theatre and Its Double (1938): theatre should shock the audience out of complacency like a plague, through sound, light, gesture, ritual, an assault on the senses, not polite dialogue.
  • ‘Cruelty’ for Artaud means rigour, intensity, necessity, not just violence.
  • In Britain: Peter Brook and Charles Marowitz's 1964 RSC ‘Theatre of Cruelty’ season; Brook's Marat/Sade (1964).
  • Bond is also close to Brecht: he wants shock to make the audience think about society (his own term: ‘aggro-effects’), not just to feel.

Censorship history

  • The Lord Chamberlain refused a licence unless the stoning scene was cut. The Royal Court staged it as a private club performance (1965, dir. William Gaskill). The theatre's management was prosecuted in 1966 and found guilty (with only a token penalty).
  • The scandal fed the campaign that ended state censorship of plays with the Theatres Act 1968.

The play

  • Thirteen scenes, South London, working class, terse dialect.
  • Len: a young man, decent, drifting; becomes Pam's family's lodger. Pam: careless, bored, obsessed with Fred. Harry and Mary: Pam's parents, who haven't spoken to each other in years. Fred: Pam's lover, probably the baby's father; attractive and indifferent. Pete, Colin, Mike, Barry: Fred's mates.
  • Len and Pam get together; Pam drops him for Fred; she has a baby she neglects (dosing it with aspirin to stop it crying). Len stays anyway.
  • Scene 6: in the park, Pam leaves the pram with Fred. Bored, the gang start teasing the baby, then pinch, punch, smear it with its own nappy, and stone it to death. Fred throws too. Pam returns and wheels the pram away without looking. Len saw it from hiding.
  • Fred goes to prison; Pam still wants him; he no longer wants her. Tensions at home (Mary and Len, a fight with a teapot); Harry talks to Len about the war and asks him to stay.
  • Final scene: almost silent. Len mends a broken chair; Pam reads; Mary clears up; Harry does his football coupon. Bond called the play ‘almost irresponsibly optimistic’ because Len, a good man, stays and tries to mend.

Themes

  • Violence as social product: the killers are bored, poor, emotionally starved, not monsters. Bond compared the baby's death to the ‘acceptable’ violence of war (strategic bombing) that society approves.
  • Apathy and moral emptiness: no one reacts properly, Pam included; Len watches.
  • Family breakdown and silence: Harry and Mary's years of not speaking.
  • Inarticulacy: characters can't express feeling; language is poor and violent.
  • Hope: Len's persistence, the mended chair. Small, but real.

What they’ll ask‘Why does Bond put the stoning on stage?’ Artaud's shock + Brecht's thinking audience: we're forced to ask what society produced these young men. Contrast with Look Back in Anger's domestic anger: violence has become social.

Drama // Write

Long essay template

  1. Intro (3–4 sentences): context → the key term in the question → your thesis (an argument, not a summary).
  2. Three body paragraphs: point → evidence (short quote or precise scene) → analysis → link back to the question.
  3. Comparing two texts? Compare inside each paragraph.
  4. Conclusion: answer the question, then one wider point.
  5. 40 minutes: 5 plan, 30 write, 5 proofread.

Short-answer template

  1. Define or identify in one sentence.
  2. One specific example from a text.
  3. Why it matters.
  4. Stop at 3–5 sentences.

Short answers (model answers)

  1. What is the Theatre of the Absurd?
    A term coined by Martin Esslin (1961) for post-war plays (Beckett, Ionesco, Genet) that show the meaninglessness of the human condition through their form: circular plots, no clear exposition, broken or repetitive language and minimal settings. It draws on Camus' idea of the absurd but presents it rather than arguing it, e.g. Waiting for Godot.
  2. Who were the ‘Angry Young Men’?
    A 1950s label for British writers, such as John Osborne, Kingsley Amis, John Braine and Alan Sillitoe, who expressed working- and lower-middle-class anger at the class system, the Establishment and post-war disillusion. Look Back in Anger (Royal Court, 1956) is the key play.
  3. What is ‘comedy of menace’?
    Irving Wardle's 1958 term for Pinter's early plays: comic, everyday situations in an enclosed room invaded by an unexplained threat. Exposition is withheld, language becomes a weapon, and the menace is never fully explained, as in The Birthday Party.
  4. Why was Saved controversial?
    Because of Scene 6, where a gang stones a baby to death in a park. The Lord Chamberlain refused a licence, the Royal Court staged it as a private club performance, and it was prosecuted. The scandal helped end stage censorship with the Theatres Act 1968.
  5. What were the aims of the Irish Literary Revival in theatre?
    To create a national Irish drama based on Irish life, folklore, myth and Hiberno-English speech, replacing the comic ‘stage Irishman’. Yeats, Lady Gregory and Martyn founded the Irish Literary Theatre (1899), leading to the Abbey (1904). Synge's Riders to the Sea dramatises Aran Island peasant life with tragic dignity.
  6. Explain the Interlude in The Kitchen.
    In the afternoon break between the lunch and dinner services, the rushing stops and the workers talk about their lives and what they dream of. Most can hardly imagine anything better. The pause shows how the work has emptied their inner lives, which prepares for Peter's breakdown.

Essay questions with battle plans

  1. Discuss Waiting for Godot as a Theatre of the Absurd play.
    1. Thesis: Beckett doesn't argue that life is absurd. He makes the audience experience it through circular form, broken language and endless waiting.
    2. Context: post-war Europe; Sartre, Camus' absurd; Esslin's term.
    3. Form: two mirroring acts, no exposition, minimal set (road, tree), the ending where nobody moves.
    4. Language: repetition, small talk to fill time, Lucky's speech as collapse.
    5. Themes: waiting, God/Godot, time and memory, power (Pozzo/Lucky).
    6. Tragicomedy: music-hall clowning vs despair.
    7. Conclusion: meaninglessness made theatrical, yet the friendship of Didi and Gogo is a small human answer.
  2. Was Look Back in Anger a revolution in British theatre?
    1. Thesis: revolutionary in voice, subject and audience; conventional in form.
    2. Context: 1956, Royal Court, Suez, welfare state, Angry Young Men, Rattigan's theatre.
    3. New: working-class provincial anti-hero, ironing board and attic flat, long rants, contemporary class anger.
    4. Old: three-act realist structure, single set, reconciliation ending.
    5. Problems: misogyny; Alison's suffering as the price of the ending.
    6. Conclusion: it changed who and what could be on stage, opening the way for Wesker and the kitchen-sink writers.
  3. How does Pinter create menace in The Birthday Party?
    1. Thesis: menace comes from what is withheld: identities, motives, the past. It grows inside comic, ordinary talk.
    2. Comedy of menace: Wardle's term; room vs intruders.
    3. Withheld exposition: who are Goldberg and McCann? What did Stanley do?
    4. Language: the interrogation; clichés; pauses.
    5. Stage images: the toy drum, blind man's buff, the blackout, Stanley transformed and silent.
    6. Meaning: conformity enforced by institutions (family, religion, ‘the organisation’).
    7. Conclusion: a comedy that turns into a nightmare of social power.
  4. Compare Riders to the Sea and Saved: death, community and silence.
    1. Thesis: both end with nearly silent acceptance after a child or son's death, but Synge's killer is nature and Bond's is society.
    2. Synge: Irish Revival, the sea as fate, women's grief, ritual (keening, holy water), poetic language.
    3. Bond: the stoning, apathy, deprivation; Artaud's shock and Brecht's thinking audience; censorship.
    4. Endings: Maurya's ‘we must be satisfied’ vs Len mending the chair.
    5. Form: one-act tragedy vs thirteen fragmented scenes.
    6. Conclusion: from tragic fate to social responsibility, a shift in 20th-century drama.
  5. Kitchen-sink realism in Look Back in Anger and The Kitchen.
    1. Thesis: both bring working-class life on stage, but Osborne focuses on one angry man at home while Wesker shows a whole workforce trapped in a system.
    2. Definition: kitchen-sink drama, Royal Court, late 1950s.
    3. Osborne: attic flat, ironing board, personal and class rage.
    4. Wesker: the Tivoli kitchen, ensemble, rhythm of work, the Interlude, Peter's breakdown, Marango.
    5. Politics: Jimmy has ‘no causes left’; Wesker's Marxist critique of alienated labour.
    6. Conclusion: realism moving from the individual to the social.
  6. Define comedy of manners and sentimental drama and explain how they differ.
    1. Definitions with dates: Restoration wit (1660–1700) vs early-18th-c. moral comedy.
    2. Causes: reopened theatres and court culture vs Collier's attack and middle-class morality.
    3. Examples: Congreve vs Steele/Cibber; Goldsmith's essay; Sheridan and Wilde as later wit.
    4. Differences: aim, class, tone, hero (use the table).
    5. Conclusion: a lasting tension in comedy between laughter and moral instruction.
